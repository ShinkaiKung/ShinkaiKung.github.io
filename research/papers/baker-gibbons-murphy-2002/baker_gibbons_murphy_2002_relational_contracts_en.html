George Baker · Robert Gibbons · Kevin J. Murphy — a repeated-game theory of why the same informal promise can be sustainable inside a firm but not between firms, or vice versa.

One-sentence summary
Integration matters not because it makes previously unverifiable outcomes verifiable, but because it changes what each party can gain by reneging on an informal promise.

1 · Framing and Literature Position

The paper starts from a broad empirical premise: organizations run on both formal structure and informal understandings. The theoretical task is to explain how the formal choice of ownership changes the informal agreements that can actually be sustained.

1.1 What is a relational contract here?

A relational contract is an informal agreement enforced by the value of future dealings rather than by a court. Inside firms, examples include unwritten understandings about assignments, promotion, termination, compensation, transfer pricing, auditing, and capital budgeting. Between firms, the paper points to long-run supplier relationships, horizontal networks, strategic alliances, joint ventures, and business groups.

The paper makes the horizontal-network examples concrete: it mentions networks of firms in the fashion industry and the diamond trade, alongside alliances, joint ventures, business groups, and “virtual” firms. These are substantive examples of relational governance outside a single legal firm, not merely decorative illustrations.

Terminology note. The same family of arrangements is sometimes called self-enforcing or implicit contracting. The authors deliberately use relational contract, following the legal literature associated with Macneil, and caution that “implicit” can misleadingly sound vague even though such understandings may be very precise.

The advantage over a formal contract is flexibility: the parties may condition behavior on outcomes they can observe after the fact even if those outcomes are too costly to specify ex ante or cannot be verified to a third party. The corresponding disadvantage is self-enforcement: every promised payment must survive the temptation to exploit the current realization and abandon the relationship.

Formal contract
Third-party enforceable; must be written in verifiable terms. It can survive even if a party would like to renege ex post.
Relational contract
Can use rich mutually observed information, but only if continuation value makes honoring the promise privately optimal.
Source: §I · pp. 39–41.

2 · Economic Environment

2.1 Agents, asset, technology, and ownership

There is one upstream party, one downstream party, and one long-lived asset. Both parties are risk neutral and use interest rate \(r\) per period. The upstream party can use the asset each period to produce one perishable good.

The good has two possible uses. Its value to downstream is \(Q\); its alternative-use value is \(P\). The maintained ordering is

Value orderingEconomic environment
\[ P_L < P_H < Q_L < Q_H. \]
Trade with downstream is always ex post efficient, even when upstream owns the asset.

Ownership of the asset conveys ownership of the good. If downstream owns the asset, he can take the good without buying it. If upstream owns the asset, she can withhold the good and use it elsewhere, so downstream must bargain to acquire it. This formal right is the bridge from ownership to reneging incentives.

Why is downstream use always more valuable? The authors give two interpretations: there may be other similar downstream users but the asset is tailored to this particular downstream party; or upstream could try to become a downstream producer herself but lacks the expertise to do so efficiently.

The “asset” can be interpreted narrowly as legal title to the good. The paper also notes an equivalent contracting interpretation: downstream ownership can resemble a specific-performance obligation, while upstream ownership resembles the absence of such an obligation.

Source: §II · pp. 43–45; footnote 6.

Each period upstream chooses an \(n\)-vector of actions \(a=(a_1,\ldots,a_n)\) at cost \(c(a)\). Actions affect the probability that the downstream value is high and the probability that the alternative-use value is high:

Outcome technology§II
\[ Q=\begin{cases}Q_H&\text{with probability }q(a)\\Q_L&\text{with probability }1-q(a)\end{cases},\qquad P=\begin{cases}P_H&\text{with probability }p(a)\\P_L&\text{with probability }1-p(a)\end{cases}. \]
Conditional on actions, \(Q\) and \(P\) are independent. The normalization is \(c(0)=q(0)=p(0)=0\). Define \(\Delta Q=Q_H-Q_L\) and \(\Delta P=P_H-P_L\).
Contracting friction
Actions are unobservable, so they cannot be directly contracted on. \(Q\) and \(P\) are observable to both parties but nonverifiable to a third party, so court-enforced contracts cannot condition on them. A relational contract can condition on them only through self-enforcement.

Formal contracts are not claimed to be absent. The model interprets \(Q\) and \(P\) as the noncontractible elements of exchange and implicitly leaves contractible elements to formal contracts. As an illustration, the paper notes that even the roughly 1,700-page General Motors–UAW agreement had gaps covered by informal understandings. The simplification therefore omits potentially important interactions between formal and relational terms.

Source: §II · pp. 44–45; footnote 7.

2.3 First-best benchmark

Because \(Q>P\) in every realization, the socially efficient use is always downstream. The first-best action vector therefore maximizes expected downstream value minus action cost.

Equation (1) · First-best surplusCore benchmark
\[ a^* \in \arg\max_a\{Q_L+q(a)\Delta Q-c(a)\},\qquad S^* = Q_L+q(a^*)\Delta Q-c(a^*). \tag{1} \]
What it says. Only the effect of actions on downstream value belongs in social surplus; raising \(P\) matters only through private bargaining incentives, not through efficient use.
Why it matters. Any effort devoted purely to raising the outside value \(P\) is socially wasteful in the baseline because the good still ends up downstream.
Source: §II · equation (1) · p. 44.

2.4 Linear multitask example and its nested cases

For intuition the authors introduce a two-action linear technology, later used in Section IV:

Two-dimensional technology§II
\[ q(a)=q_1a_1+q_2a_2,\qquad p(a)=p_1a_1+p_2a_2,\qquad q_i,p_i\ge0. \]
Case 1

One-dimensional effort

\(q_2=p_2=0\). The same action affects both downstream and alternative-use outcomes. Outside-market incentives can partially align with productivity.

Case 2

Unproductive multitasking

\(q_2=p_1=0\). Action \(a_1\) raises \(Q\); action \(a_2\) raises only \(P\). Effort on \(a_2\) is privately useful for bargaining but socially wasteful.

Case 3

Mixed task values

For academics, for example, research may increase both internal and external value while administration may increase only internal value. General \(q_i,p_i\) capture such task-specific effects.

Source: §II · pp. 45–46.

3 · Four Governance Forms

Section III solves the four cells of Figure I. The key payoff comparison is not simply “market versus firm.” It is how ownership changes the fallback from a relational promise.

3.1 Spot Outsourcing

Upstream owns the asset and there is no relational contract. After \(Q_i,P_j\) realize, the parties bargain over the good. With equal Nash bargaining power, downstream pays

Spot bargaining price§III.A
\[ \text{price}_{ij}=P_j+\frac12(Q_i-P_j)=\frac12(Q_i+P_j). \]
Upstream captures the alternative-use value plus half of gains from trade.
Equation (2) · Upstream action choiceSpot Outsourcing
\[ U^{SO}=\max_a\left\{\frac12[Q_L+q(a)\Delta Q]+\frac12[P_L+p(a)\Delta P]-c(a)\right\}. \tag{2} \]
Because upstream is rewarded for raising both \(Q\) and \(P\), she may underinvest in productive \(Q\)-enhancing actions, overinvest in \(P\)-enhancing bargaining actions, or both.
Equation (3) · Total surplusSpot Outsourcing
\[ S^{SO}=D^{SO}+U^{SO}=Q_L+q(a^{SO})\Delta Q-c(a^{SO}). \tag{3} \]
Extreme example: \(\Delta Q=0\)
If upstream effort cannot raise downstream value at all, the first-best effort is zero. Yet under Spot Outsourcing upstream can still spend costly effort to raise \(P\), because a higher alternative-use value improves the Nash-bargained sale price. This stark case isolates the bargaining-position distortion.
Important distinction: specificity levels ≠ marginal incentives
The asset can be highly specific in levels (e.g. \(Q_L-P_H\) is large) yet still induce overinvestment if \(\Delta P\) is large relative to \(\Delta Q\). Investment behavior is driven by margins, not by the level measure of asset specificity alone.
Source: §III.A · pp. 48–50; equations (2)–(3); footnote 10.

3.2 Spot Employment

Downstream owns the asset and there is no relational contract. Downstream can take the good without making a noncontractible performance payment. Anticipating this, upstream chooses no costly action. Thus \(a^{SE}=0\) and

Spot Employment surplus§III.B
\[ S^{SE}=Q_L. \]
Spot employment can dominate spot outsourcing only when the actions induced by outside bargaining have negative net social value: \(q(a^{SO})\Delta Q-c(a^{SO})<0\).
Qualification
The zero-effort result is deliberately stark because formal contracts are omitted. The authors note that adding formal contracts can generate positive but generally non-first-best effort under spot employment; they suppress that channel to isolate relational contracting.
Source: §III.B · pp. 49–50.

3.3 Relational Employment

Downstream owns the asset, but now the parties use a repeated informal compensation agreement \((s,\{b_{ij}\})\). Salary \(s\) is paid at the beginning of the period; after \(Q_i,P_j\) are observed, downstream is supposed to pay bonus \(b_{ij}\). These payments can create action incentives only if both parties prefer honoring them to reneging.

Equation (4) · Upstream action under promised bonusesRelational Employment
\[ U^{RE}=\max_a \Big[ s+b_{LL}(1-q)(1-p)+b_{HL}q(1-p)+b_{LH}(1-q)p+b_{HH}qp-c(a) \Big]. \tag{4} \]
Here \(q=q(a)\) and \(p=p(a)\). Salary allocates surplus; differences across \(b_{ij}\) provide marginal incentives.
Equation (5) · SurplusRelational Employment
\[ S^{RE}=U^{RE}+D^{RE}=Q_L+q(a^{RE})\Delta Q-c(a^{RE}). \tag{5} \]

Trigger strategy and fallback

If either party reneges, the other refuses future relational contracting forever. The relationship then uses spot governance. Crucially, the parties may renegotiate asset ownership after reneging, so the fallback is whichever spot form yields higher total surplus: \(\max\{S^{SE},S^{SO}\}\). This endogenized fallback is central to the paper.

If spot employment is the efficient fallback, downstream honors a promised bonus when the current saving from refusing \(b_{ij}\) is no larger than the loss of future downstream relational surplus; upstream similarly honors a negative payment / accepts the promised payment when continuation is valuable enough.

Equations (6)–(10) · Self-enforcementRelational Employment
\[ -b_{ij}+\frac1rD^{RE}\ge \frac1rD^{SE},\tag{6} \] \[ b_{ij}+\frac1rU^{RE}\ge \frac1rU^{SE}.\tag{7} \]
\[ \max_{ij}b_{ij}-\min_{ij}b_{ij}\le \frac1r(S^{RE}-S^{SE}),\tag{8} \] \[ \max_{ij}b_{ij}-\min_{ij}b_{ij}\le \frac1r(S^{RE}-S^{SO}),\tag{9} \]
\[ \max_{ij}b_{ij}-\min_{ij}b_{ij} \le \frac1r\Big(S^{RE}-\max\{S^{SE},S^{SO}\}\Big). \tag{10} \]
Left side. Maximum total current temptation to renege across realized states. Under employment, the employee has no property-right claim to the good, so the whole variation in promised bonuses matters.
Right side. Present value of relational capital: surplus from continuing the relationship minus the best spot fallback available after breakdown.
Equations (8) and (9) are the two fallback-specific versions: subtract \(S^{SE}\) if spot employment is the fallback and subtract \(S^{SO}\) if spot outsourcing is the fallback. Equation (10) combines them.
Feasibility vs optimality
The efficient relational-employment contract maximizes \(S^{RE}\) subject to (10). Depending on primitives, it may achieve first best, achieve only a second-best improvement, or fail to improve on spot employment at all.
Source: §III.C · pp. 50–53 · equations (4)–(10); footnote 11 for generalization beyond binary \(Q,P\).

3.4 Relational Outsourcing

Upstream owns the asset and the parties promise the same kind of relational payment schedule. For any fixed \((s,\{b_{ij}\})\), the action choice and total surplus are the same as under relational employment if that contract is enforceable. The difference is the current payoff after reneging.

If downstream refuses the promised payment, he still needs the good. The parties revert immediately to the spot bargaining price \(\frac12(Q_i+P_j)\). Likewise, upstream can renege by insisting on the spot price rather than the relational price. Therefore the relevant temptation is not the level of \(b_{ij}\), but its deviation from the spot price.

Equation (11) · SurplusRelational Outsourcing
\[ S^{RO}=U^{RO}+D^{RO}=Q_L+q(a^{RO})\Delta Q-c(a^{RO}). \tag{11} \]
Equations (12)–(16) · Self-enforcementRelational Outsourcing
\[ b_{ij}-\frac12(Q_i+P_j)\le \frac1r(D^{RO}-D^{SO}), \tag{12} \] \[ b_{ij}-\frac12(Q_i+P_j)\ge \frac1r(U^{SO}-U^{RO}). \tag{13} \]
\[ \max_{ij}\left[b_{ij}-\frac12(Q_i+P_j)\right] -\min_{ij}\left[b_{ij}-\frac12(Q_i+P_j)\right] \le \frac1r(S^{RO}-S^{SO}),\tag{14} \] \[ \max_{ij}\left[b_{ij}-\frac12(Q_i+P_j)\right] -\min_{ij}\left[b_{ij}-\frac12(Q_i+P_j)\right] \le \frac1r(S^{RO}-S^{SE}).\tag{15} \]
\[ \max_{ij}\left[b_{ij}-\frac12(Q_i+P_j)\right] -\min_{ij}\left[b_{ij}-\frac12(Q_i+P_j)\right] \le \frac1r\Big(S^{RO}-\max\{S^{SO},S^{SE}\}\Big). \tag{16} \]
Equation (13) is the upstream counterpart to (12); equations (14) and (15) are the two fallback-specific combined constraints. Equation (16) is necessary and sufficient.
Recourse effect
If downstream refuses a promised bonus, an independent upstream supplier can still extract the spot value of the good through bargaining. This can make high promised payments more credible outside the firm.
Bargaining-position effect
Ownership also motivates upstream to improve \(P\), because a higher alternative-use value raises the spot bargaining price. This can distort tasks and make nonintegration worse.
Source: §III.D · pp. 53–56 · equations (11)–(16).

3.5 Main Proposition and Selective-Intervention Corollary

Main Proposition · author's argument
A given relational contract \((s,\{b_{ij}\})\) produces the same actions and total surplus under either ownership structure, but the maximum total reneging temptation is \(\max b_{ij}-\min b_{ij}\) under relational employment and \(\max[b_{ij}-\frac12(Q_i+P_j)]-\min[b_{ij}-\frac12(Q_i+P_j)]\) under relational outsourcing.
ObjectRelational EmploymentRelational Outsourcing
Who owns asset / good?DownstreamUpstream
Same promised bonus schedule inducesSame action vector and same current total surplus
Current reneging benchmarkZero transfer on owned goodSpot Nash bargaining price \(\frac12(Q_i+P_j)\)
Maximum total temptationRange of \(b_{ij}\)Range of \(b_{ij}-\frac12(Q_i+P_j)\)
ImplicationOwnership changes the feasible set of relational contracts, so ownership can be chosen to support a better relationship.
Corollary · firms cannot mimic the spot market
To reproduce spot-outsourcing incentives inside an integrated firm, the relational bonus would need to be \(b_{ij}=\frac12(Q_i+P_j)\). But once the asset is integrated, this bonus varies across states and creates a positive reneging temptation, while continuing the replicated spot outcome offers no surplus advantage over the best spot fallback. Constraint (10) therefore fails.

This gives a formal rationale for Williamson's claim that selective intervention is impossible. A firm cannot simply bring a market transaction inside, preserve all market incentives, and intervene only in states where the market would be inefficient. Ownership changes the credibility of the payments needed to reproduce the market.

The introduction explicitly frames this as a caution for attempts to “bring the market inside the firm” through empowerment, transfer pricing, or performance-measurement systems such as Economic Value Added (EVA): changing ownership can make the market-like payment rule itself noncredible.

The paper also notes the reverse: nonintegration cannot generally mimic spot employment either. And Kreps's corporate-culture logic cannot be represented by a single ownership-free repeated game if the same “culture” is meant to govern both employees and outside suppliers; the reneging problem differs by ownership.

Source: Proposition and Corollary · pp. 56–58; footnotes 12–13.

4 · Linear–Quadratic Specialization

Section IV specializes the general model to obtain clean comparative results and a phase diagram. These results are explicitly described by the authors as exploratory and suggestive: they depend on special functional forms and do not exhaust the implications of the main proposition.

4.1 Functional forms, spot actions, and restricted bonuses

Linear–quadratic environment§IV.A
\[ a=(a_1,a_2),\quad q(a)=q_1a_1+q_2a_2,\quad p(a)=p_1a_1+p_2a_2,\quad c(a)=\frac12a_1^2+\frac12a_2^2, \] \[ q_i,p_i\ge0,\qquad q_1p_2\ne q_2p_1. \]
First-best and Spot-Outsourcing actionsEquation (17)
\[ a_i^{FB}=q_i\Delta Q,\qquad a_i^{SO}=\frac12q_i\Delta Q+\frac12p_i\Delta P,\quad i=1,2. \tag{17} \]
Spot employment still gives \(a_i^{SE}=0\).

For tractability, relational bonuses are restricted to be additively separable across the two outcomes: \(b_{ij}=b_i+\beta_j\). Define \(\Delta b=b_H-b_L\) and \(\Delta\beta=\beta_H-\beta_L\). Then relational incentives map one-to-one into actions:

Equation (18) · Relational action choiceAny relational ownership regime
\[ a_1^R=q_1\Delta b+p_1\Delta\beta,\qquad a_2^R=q_2\Delta b+p_2\Delta\beta. \tag{18} \]
The restriction plus \(q_1p_2\ne q_2p_1\) makes the mapping from the two bonus slopes to the two actions invertible.
Source: §IV.A · pp. 61–62; equations (17)–(18); footnote 14.

4.2 The two self-enforcement constraints become geometric distances

Equation (10′)Relational Employment
\[ |\Delta b|+|\Delta\beta| \le \frac1r\left(S^{RE}-\max\{S^{SO},S^{SE}\}\right). \tag{10'} \]
The employment reneging temptation is the \(L^1\) distance of the relational bonus slopes from zero.
Equation (16′)Relational Outsourcing
\[ \left|\Delta b-\frac12\Delta Q\right| +\left|\Delta\beta-\frac12\Delta P\right| \le \frac1r\left(S^{RO}-\max\{S^{SO},S^{SE}\}\right). \tag{16'} \]
The outsourcing temptation is distance from the spot-bargaining incentive vector \((\frac12\Delta Q,\frac12\Delta P)\).
The geometric intuition
The same desired action vector corresponds to the same \((\Delta b,\Delta\beta)\), but “no temptation” is centered at a different point under each ownership regime: zero inside the firm versus the spot-bargaining slopes outside the firm.

First best requires \(\Delta b=\Delta Q\) and \(\Delta\beta=0\). Therefore:

Equations (19)–(20) · First-best feasibilityOwnership comparison
\[ \Delta Q \le \frac1r\Big(S^{FB}-\max\{S^{SO},S^{SE}\}\Big) \qquad\text{(relational employment),} \tag{19} \] \[ \frac12(\Delta Q+\Delta P) \le \frac1r\Big(S^{FB}-\max\{S^{SO},S^{SE}\}\Big) \qquad\text{(relational outsourcing).} \tag{20} \]
At sufficiently low \(r\), both ownership regimes can support first best. If \(\Delta P>\Delta Q\), there is an intermediate range where employment can support first best but outsourcing cannot; if \(\Delta P<\Delta Q\), the reverse can occur.
Source: §IV.A · pp. 62–63; equations (10′), (16′), (19), (20); Appendix 1.

5 · Five Comparative Results

The five results show what becomes possible once ownership is allowed to change the self-enforcement set rather than only static marginal incentives.

1

Vertical integration is an efficient response to widely varying supply prices

Author's Result

Holding \(q_1,q_2,p_1,p_2,\Delta Q,r\) fixed, there is a threshold \(\Delta P^*\) such that sufficiently large \(\Delta P\) makes downstream ownership efficient. Under relational outsourcing, \(P\) directly enters the current reneging benchmark because the owner can threaten alternative use. Under relational employment, the downstream owner already owns the good, so \(P\) drops out of the reneging temptation.

The economic mechanism is ex post, not risk sharing. All parties are risk neutral. A high market price tempts upstream to break the relational deal; a low market price tempts downstream. Wide price variation therefore makes a promised interfirm relational price difficult to sustain.

Connection to Carlton's puzzle
The result offers an explanation for why risk-neutral firms may integrate for “certainty of supply”: not because uncertainty itself is costly, but because volatile outside prices undermine the relational contract.
Source: Result 1 · pp. 64–65; proof in Appendix 2, Lemmas 1–3 · pp. 76–77; footnotes 15–16.
2

High-powered incentives create bigger reneging temptations under integration

Author's Result

If the efficient relational-employment contract is below first best on both actions, then any feasible efficient relational-outsourcing contract uses stronger action incentives; otherwise relational outsourcing is infeasible. Intuitively, an employee has little recourse if the firm refuses a promised performance payment, whereas an independent supplier can fall back on bargaining over the good.

With strong desired incentives, \(\Delta b>\frac12\Delta Q\) and \(\Delta\beta>\frac12\Delta P\), the outsourcing temptation in (16′) is smaller than the employment temptation in (10′). Hence a larger promised performance payment can be credible between firms than inside an otherwise equivalent firm.

Potentially testable prediction
Measured performance-contingent payments should be lower for employees than for comparable independent contractors in environments where both forms coexist. This translates Williamson's “higher-powered market incentives” into a prediction about measurable payment levels.
Counterintuitive R2 mechanism: why reward an unproductive task?

In the unproductive-multitasking example, the relational-employment optimum on the R2 boundary has \(\Delta\beta^{RE}=0\). Relational outsourcing instead may set \(\Delta\beta^{RO}>0\), inducing some socially wasteful effort on \(a_2\). Why? Starting from zero \(a_2\), the direct surplus loss is second order, while moving the relational payment closer to the spot-bargaining schedule reduces reneging temptation at first order. That slack allows a larger \(\Delta b\), stronger productive \(a_1\), and a higher feasible total surplus.

Source: Result 2 · pp. 65–67; proof p. 77; Figure II boundary R2.
3

The optimal integration decision can depend on the discount rate

Author's Result

At low \(r\), future relational surplus is valuable enough that both ownership regimes can support first-best incentives. But first-best generates different current reneging temptations under the two regimes, so as \(r\) rises one form can lose first-best feasibility earlier than the other. At still higher \(r\), relational contracts can disappear and a spot form becomes efficient.

This comparative static is intrinsically repeated-game. A purely static asset-ownership model has no discount rate governing the value of future cooperation.

Source: Result 3 · p. 67; equations (19)–(20); proof pp. 77–78; Figure II arrow R3.
4

Holding marginal incentives fixed, payoff levels can change optimal integration

Author's Result

Hold \(q_i\Delta Q+p_i\Delta P\) fixed for \(i=1,2\). This holds constant the marginal incentives governing spot outsourcing and therefore holds fixed the static comparison of spot outsourcing and spot employment. Now change \(\Delta P\) or \(\Delta Q\), adjusting \(p_i\) or \(q_i\) to preserve those margins.

The spot actions do not change, but the levels of outside payoffs entering reneging temptations do. Consequently the feasible relational-contract set changes and the efficient ownership structure can reverse. This is a sharp departure from canonical Grossman–Hart-style static logic where, under their benchmark bargaining structure, payoff levels have no separate role once marginal incentives are fixed.

Qualification
The authors explicitly note that not every static ownership theory makes payoff levels irrelevant; changing the bargaining game, as in De Meza–Lockwood, can alter that property.
Source: Result 4 · pp. 67–68; proof p. 78; footnote 17.
5

A mediocre spot market may prevent the existence of any firm

Author's Result

Suppose spot outsourcing is better than spot employment, \(S^{SO}>S^{SE}\), but a relational-employment arrangement could in principle create still more surplus. The spot market remains the fallback after any internal relational breakdown. If that fallback is attractive enough relative to the relational gain, the future loss from reneging is too small to support the payments needed for improvement.

The striking logic is that the market need not beat the firm in a direct efficiency contest. The availability of the market can make the firm infeasible by weakening the punishment for breaking the internal relational contract.

6 · Appendices and Proof Logic

The paper's appendices are not merely mechanical. Appendix 1 exposes the geometry of the reneging constraints; Appendix 2 shows exactly why the five comparative results follow and adds a symmetric sixth result.

6.1 Appendix 1 — deriving (10′) and (16′)

Relational Employment: four sign cases collapse to \(|\Delta b|+|\Delta\beta|\)
CaseSignsMax–min bonus range
1\(\Delta b>0,\ \Delta\beta>0\)\(\Delta b+\Delta\beta\)
2\(\Delta b>0,\ \Delta\beta<0\)\(\Delta b-\Delta\beta\)
3\(\Delta b<0,\ \Delta\beta>0\)\(-\Delta b+\Delta\beta\)
4\(\Delta b<0,\ \Delta\beta<0\)\(-\Delta b-\Delta\beta\)

All four are \(|\Delta b|+|\Delta\beta|\), yielding (10′).

Relational Outsourcing: four sign cases around the spot-price slopes

Define \(Z=b_L+\beta_L-\frac12(Q_L+P_L)\). Then each state-specific deviation from the spot price can be written as \(Z\) plus combinations of \(\Delta b-\frac12\Delta Q\) and \(\Delta\beta-\frac12\Delta P\). The four sign cases therefore collapse to

\[ \left|\Delta b-\frac12\Delta Q\right|+\left|\Delta\beta-\frac12\Delta P\right|, \]

which yields (16′). This derivation makes transparent that the outsourcing “zero temptation” point is the spot-bargaining incentive vector, not zero.

7 · Discussion and Applications

Section V broadens the paper from a two-party model to an organizational principle: formal structure and informal relationships interact, so formal design should be chosen partly for the relational contracts it makes possible.

7.1 Formal versus informal organizational structure

Organizational sociology has long distinguished formal job descriptions, reporting relationships, and legal contracts from informal norms, mutual understandings, and social networks. The paper identifies asset ownership as a formal element and relational contracting as an informal element.

The economic value added is not merely to say that formal and informal structures coexist. It is to model their interaction and therefore make the formal structure an optimization variable: choose ownership to facilitate the most productive informal agreement.

Broader principle
Choose formal organizational structures while accounting for how they alter informal self-enforcement. The authors explicitly suggest extending this principle beyond ownership and beyond relational contracts.
Source: §V.A · pp. 70–71; footnote 20.

7.2 Joint ventures, alliances, networks, and business groups

The four Figure-I forms do not exhaust organizational variety. The authors point to joint ventures, strategic alliances, networks, business groups, clans, and virtual organizations, and argue that economists have often emphasized only formal ownership aspects of these structures.

The conjecture is that their distinctive ownership patterns may be partly valuable because they support distinctive relational contracts. A joint venture could be modeled as two parties at one production stage creating and jointly controlling an asset at another stage. A business group could involve multiple parties and stages linked by both cross-ownership and relational contracts, perhaps coordinated through a central party.

Thus 50–50 joint-venture ownership or minority cross-holdings need not be treated as imperfect compromises on a one-dimensional market–hierarchy continuum; they can be formal structures selected to sustain particular informal relations.

Source: §V.B · pp. 71–72; footnote 21.

7.3 Internal organizational processes: transfer pricing and corporate governance

Early transfer-pricing theory often tried to recreate market prices inside the firm. Eccles's field evidence instead found that transfer prices often serve performance measurement and incentive roles while allocation rights remain centralized. This fits the model: under relational employment, internal transfers do not allocate ownership of the good—the firm already owns it. They reward actions that make the good more valuable downstream.

Executive compensation is not fully mechanical. Boards retain discretion over timing and size of payouts and combine objective with subjective performance measures. The paper interprets the board as helping design and implement a relational contract between management and shareholders, using judgment to separate management action from luck and to correct distortions in accounting measures.

The authors also point to capital budgeting and compensation more generally. Subjective judgment and reputation can be central even when the surrounding process looks formally contractual. Formal design—functional versus divisional organization, individual versus team jobs, and so on—may facilitate or impede these informal arrangements.

Source: §V.C · pp. 72–73; footnote 22.

7.4 Management and the nature of the firm

The paper closes with a role for managers that is largely absent from many theories of the firm. Managers design the relevant relational contract, communicate expectations, assess noncontractible outcomes, decide whether promises should be honored, and sometimes change the relational contract itself.

Changing a relational contract is especially difficult: management must terminate one understanding without destroying the credibility required to start another, and the new policy may look like reneging on the old one.

The authors do not claim that management occurs only inside firms. Ongoing supply relationships also require management. Nor do they claim that every firm needs a manager: a simple Grossman–Hart-style firm could concentrate all noncontractible rights in one owner. Their claim is that as noncontractible rights become numerous and complex, their exercise must be coordinated through relational contracts, creating an essential managerial function.

Source: §V.D · pp. 73–74.

9 · Critical Reading

Key assumptions

  • Two parties and one asset, infinitely repeated stationary interaction.
  • Risk neutrality and common interest rate \(r\).
  • Upstream actions are privately observed; \(Q,P\) are observed by both but nonverifiable.
  • \(P_L<P_H<Q_L<Q_H\), so downstream use is always ex post efficient.
  • Asset ownership conveys ownership of the produced good.
  • Spot outsourcing uses equal-power Nash bargaining.
  • After reneging, relational contracting ends forever and ownership can be renegotiated to the efficient spot form.
  • Section IV uses linear production, quadratic cost, and additive \(Q/P\) bonus components.

What actually drives the results?

  • Main proposition: current reneging payoffs differ by ownership.
  • Result 1: \(\Delta P\) enters outsourcing temptation but not employment temptation.
  • Result 2: nonintegrated supplier retains spot bargaining recourse.
  • Result 3: continuation surplus is scaled by \(1/r\).
  • Result 4: self-enforcement depends on payoff levels, not only marginal action incentives.
  • Result 5: punishment value is endogenous—the best spot form remains available after relational breakdown.

Authors' own qualifications and boundaries

The paper repeatedly marks the boundary of its claims. Formal contracts are deliberately suppressed, even though contractible and noncontractible elements can interact. Results 1–5 are derived in a special linear–quadratic environment and are described as exploratory. The baseline punishment structure is a two-party trigger strategy; broader reputational markets are not modeled. Appendix results depend on the chosen functional form and bonus restriction even though the main proposition is much more general.

Analytical limitations

Analytical Synthesis

Punishment architecture

Permanent reversion to efficient spot governance is analytically clean but strong. With partial forgiveness, third-party reputations, or new partners, continuation threats and therefore integration incentives could differ.

Ownership is binary

Many real forms use shared ownership, minority stakes, option rights, or contingent control. The discussion anticipates these forms but the model does not solve them.

Formal contracting is held in the background

If formal contracts alter the outside option, information, or feasible bonus schedule, formal and relational instruments may be complements or substitutes.

Exogenous relationship

Partner search, matching, switching costs, relationship formation, and endogenous termination are absent. These could jointly determine relational capital and firm boundaries.

Exogenous bargaining protocol

Equal Nash bargaining pins down the spot price. Different bargaining power can shift the “zero temptation” point under outsourcing.

Empirical mapping

The payment-level prediction is suggestive, but observed employee–contractor comparisons face sorting, task heterogeneity, measurement, and endogenous organizational form.

External Validity

Analytical Synthesis

The mechanism maps most directly to bilateral, repeated relationships in which important performance outcomes are mutually observed but difficult for courts to verify, and in which ownership materially changes the parties' fallback rights over the traded good. The paper itself does not establish that every observed firm boundary is governed by this channel.

Applications to joint ventures, alliances, business groups, transfer pricing, and corporate governance are explicitly presented as extensions or interpretations rather than solved versions of the baseline model. Likewise, Results 1–5 use the linear–quadratic specialization, so their qualitative reach should be separated from the more general main proposition.

Open Questions

  • How should formal organizational structures beyond ownership be chosen when they alter informal self-enforcement?
  • How do joint ownership, minority stakes, cross-ownership, alliances, and business groups change reneging temptations and relational capital?
  • How can managers credibly change a relational contract without making the new arrangement look like reneging on the old one?
  • How do formal contracts interact with the noncontractible dimensions \(Q,P\) that the baseline deliberately isolates?
Source: §V.A–D · pp. 70–74; footnotes 20–22. Questions are organized from the authors' discussion; where phrased more generally, they are learning-oriented synthesis.

9.1 Research Implications / Extensions

Research Extension

Endogenous governance transitions

Let ownership change over time as relational capital, outside prices, or partner quality evolve. The paper gives a natural state variable—credibility / continuation surplus—but does not model dynamic ownership adjustment.

Shared and partial ownership

Replace the binary allocation with joint ventures, minority equity, or option rights, and ask how partial recourse reshapes the reneging set.

Search and matching

Allow parties to meet alternative partners. A richer outside market changes both bargaining payoffs and the punishment from relational breakdown.

Formal × relational contract design

Endogenize which dimensions become formally verifiable and how formal clauses change the credible informal bonus schedule.

Network relational capital

In business groups or production networks, reneging against one party may damage reputation with others, making organizational boundaries a network problem.

Empirical tests of fallback dependence

Use shocks to outside-option values or supply-price dispersion and test whether relationship breakdown, bonus sensitivity, or integration responds in the direction predicted by the model.

13 · What Should I Remember?

  1. Relational contracts use mutually observed but nonverifiable information and are enforced by future surplus.
  2. Ownership does not change what a fixed relational bonus asks upstream to do; it changes how tempting it is to break that promise.
  3. Under employment, temptation is the range of promised bonuses. Under outsourcing, temptation is the range of promised bonuses net of spot bargaining prices.
  4. The best punishment is not “zero.” After relational breakdown, parties can use the better spot governance form. Thus relational capital is surplus above an endogenous fallback.
  5. This makes integration an instrument in the service of the relationship: choose ownership partly to support the better informal contract.
  6. A firm cannot simply “bring the market inside” because the spot-market payment schedule is not self-enforcing after ownership changes.
  7. High \(\Delta P\) can favor integration; strong performance incentives can favor outsourcing; patience and payoff levels can change ownership even when static margins do not.
  8. A sufficiently attractive fallback market can make an otherwise more efficient firm impossible to sustain.
  9. The broader organizational lesson is formal × informal complementarity: ownership, joint stakes, boards, transfer-pricing systems, and other formal devices should be evaluated by the relational contracts they facilitate.
Most important equation pair
\((10)\) versus \((16)\): same continuation-surplus logic, different current reneging temptation because ownership changes the spot fallback in the current transaction.
Biggest contribution
A formal theory that distinguishes relational contracting inside and outside firms and makes the firm boundary partly a choice of which informal agreements can be credibly sustained.
Biggest limitation
The core proposition is broad, but the five comparative results rely on a deliberately stylized two-party punishment environment and a linear–quadratic specialization with restricted bonuses.
Biggest open question
How do multiple partners, search, shared ownership, formal contracting, and endogenous changes in relational capital jointly determine governance transitions over time?
