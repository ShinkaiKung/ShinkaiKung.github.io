Strategic Alliances: Bridges Between “Islands of Conscious Power”

George P. Baker · Robert Gibbons · Kevin J. Murphy · March 2008

JEL D2L14L22Strategic alliancesTheory of the Firm

The paper asks how firms can coordinate a joint project without collapsing the problem into a simple market-versus-integration choice. Its answer is to treat governance as an allocation of assets, decision rights, and payoffs under two central frictions: spillovers to the parent firms and ex post noncontractibility of decisions.

AUTHOR'S ARGUMENT

Strategic alliances are contractual “bridges” across firm boundaries. Different bridges make different parties internalize different pieces of surplus, so many organizational forms can be second-best; there is no universal winner.

Source: Abstract and §1 · PDF pp. 1–6 · source map

Paper Dashboard · Paper at a Glance

Research Question

How should parties allocate assets, decision rights, and payoffs when a joint project creates state-dependent spillovers on both parents?

Core Friction

Important implementation decisions are not contractible ex post; the state is observable but not verifiable, and the opportunity is too fleeting for renegotiation.

Mechanism

Governance changes who controls decisions and which payoffs that controller internalizes, changing the subset of states in which the project proceeds.

Evidence

≈12,500 publicly disclosed pharma–biotech alliance contracts, 1973–2001, document dense networks and a wide menu of governance forms.

Central Result

Each feasible governance structure considered can be optimal for some environment, but in general none implements the first best.

Identification

1. The Puzzle: From “Islands” to Contractual Bridges

1.1 The market–firm dichotomy is too coarse

Strategic alliances range from unstructured collaborations, consortia, and joint ventures to transactions that alter asset ownership. Pharmaceuticals, biotechnology, medical devices, airlines, and telecommunications display more governance forms than a simple market-versus-firm dichotomy can describe.

The title borrows the image of firms as “islands of conscious power.” The paper does not reject firm boundaries; it argues that an adequate map of production also needs the contractual bridges that transfer decision rights and payoffs across those boundaries.

Island

Sharp boundary; authority and ownership concentrated inside the firm.

Bridge

Contractual allocation of decision rights/payoffs across fixed boundaries.

Hybrid

Joint ventures and related forms superimpose new governance on existing firms.

Source: §1 · PDF pp. 2–5

1.2 What practitioners emphasized

AUTHOR'S ARGUMENT

Spillovers / externalities: the joint project can help or hurt the parent firms’ core activities.

AUTHOR'S ARGUMENT

Ex post contracting problems: governance must induce efficient behavior after the state is known because the key decisions often cannot be contracted.

QUALIFICATION

Specific-investment hold-up and bargaining over returns remain standard organizational-economics mechanisms, but they played a markedly smaller role in the practitioner discussions that motivated this paper.

Source: §1 · PDF pp. 2–3

3. The Model: Assets, Spillovers, and Ex Post Control

3.1 Economic Environment

There are four assets, {A, a, B, b}, and initially two firms. Firm A owns its core asset A and project asset a; Firm B owns core asset B and project asset b. Assets a and b create direct profits only when they are used together in a coordinated way.

Assets and decisions

Each project asset i∈{a,b} has a decision right di and a direct payoff πi. Coordinated use yields πa>0 and πb>0; other uses yield zero.

State and spillovers

The observable-but-unverifiable state s generates πA(s) and πB(s), private benefits to the parent firms that may be positive or negative and are jointly distributed by F, with finite supports [π̲A, π̄A] and [π̲B, π̄B] in the paper’s notation.

CONTRACTIBILITY LADDER

State s: observable, not verifiable. Spillovers πA,πB: observable private benefits, not contractible. Decisions: not contractible. Decision rights: potentially contractible and transferable. Direct project payoffs πa,πb: initially tied to assets, then made contractible in §3e to permit royalties.

Source: §3a · PDF pp. 8–10; Figure 3 · PDF p. 9

3.2 Timing and the Ex Post Contracting Problem

1
Choose governance ex ante

Allocate decision rights and payoffs through ownership and/or contracts; side payments may accompany changes.

2
State s is revealed

The parties observe the realized spillovers.

3
Controllers choose da, db

Each decision-right holder acts according to her own payoff criterion.

4
Payoffs accrue

Asset owners or royalty holders receive direct payoffs; parents receive their private spillovers.

ASSUMPTION 1 · FLEETING OPPORTUNITY

After s is revealed, there is not enough time to recontract over decision rights or payoffs before action must be taken.

ASSUMPTION 2 · NONCONTRACTIBLE DECISIONS

The actions themselves cannot be contracted before or after s. Hence ex post Coasian bargaining over the decision is ruled out.

3.4 First Best and Why the Main Analysis Excludes Full Mergers

First-best implementation criterion
πA(s) + πB(s) + πa + πb > 0

What it says. Implement exactly when total social surplus from the joint project—including both parent spillovers—is positive.

A merger of parent firms A and B could internalize all four payoff components. The paper deliberately sets that option aside in the main alliance analysis: a and b are envisioned as small relative to core assets A and B, while merging the parents would incur unmodeled integration costs such as harmonizing control systems and cultures, overcoming communication barriers, and moving decision makers farther from consequences.

QUALIFICATION

The exclusion is not a claim that mergers are never efficient. The paper returns to this point in §4: if a and b are large enough relative to A and B, the inefficiency of the best second-best alliance can exceed the cost of integrating the parents, making merger attractive.

Alternative interpretation in footnote 5

The authors note another way to rationalize the same restriction: πA(s) and πB(s) can be viewed as inalienable private benefits flowing to A and B. Under that interpretation, it is literally impossible to place all decision rights and all payoffs under one party, so full internalization cannot be achieved simply by reallocating the project assets.

Source: §3b · PDF pp. 11–12, including footnote 5; end of §4 · PDF p. 26

4. Defining Governance Structures

4.1 Asset Ownership: Four Families, Nine Allocations

In §3c, ownership of a project asset bundles its decision right and direct payoff. Reallocating a and b therefore changes both control and the payoff that a controller internalizes. Even with only two transferable project assets, permutations create nine distinct allocations.

Unstructured Collaboration

A and B retain separate project assets (or swap them). With A owning a and B owning b, both must voluntarily cooperate, so implementation requires πA+πa>0 and πB+πb>0.

Acquisition

One parent owns both project assets. Acquisition by A makes A control both decisions and receive both direct project payoffs; B's spillover does not enter A's implementation criterion.

Total Divestiture

A and B transfer a and b to third party C. Because πa,πb>0, C wants implementation in every state and ignores both parents' spillovers.

Strategic Divestiture

Only one project asset is moved to C. One parent therefore remains a veto player while C always favors proceeding. Asset swaps can produce symmetric variants.

4.2 Contracting Over Decision Rights: Licensing

The next step separates a decision right from asset ownership. A contract may give another party the right to decide whether or how an asset is used while the asset owner keeps its direct payoff. The paper calls this a license agreement.

IMPORTANT DISTINCTION

Decision ≠ decision right. The realized action itself remains noncontractible. What can be contracted is who has authority to take that action after s is observed.

4.3 Contracting Over Payoffs: Royalty Agreements

A still richer governance menu becomes possible if direct project payoffs can be contracted after outcomes are observed. The authors first reject a different idea: allocating an entire noncontractible payoff stream away from the party holding residual decision rights is implausible because that controller could divert unverifiable returns. This follows the logic emphasized by Holmstrom and Tirole (1989).

MODELING CHOICE

The paper therefore makes πa and πb contractible, while keeping πA(s) and πB(s) noncontractible private benefits. This supports royalty shares without undoing the core spillover problem.

5. Efficient Governance: Comparing Implementation Regions

5.1 One Geometry, Many Decision Rules

Write x=πA(s), y=πB(s), and f(x,y) for their joint density. Every governance structure corresponds to a region of the (x,y) plane in which the project is implemented. Expected surplus is total project surplus, x+y+πa+πb, integrated over that governance structure's implementation region.

Expected surplus · equivalent compact form of the paper's §4 integrals
S(x,y) = x + y + πa + πb
Vg = ∬𝓘g S(x,y) f(x,y) dx dy

Interpretation. 𝓘g is the implementation region generated by governance form g. The paper writes the corresponding value for First Best, Unstructured Collaboration, Acquisition, Total/Strategic Divestiture, Licensing, and the two royalty structures as explicit integrals with governance-specific bounds. This compact expression is algebraically equivalent and makes the comparison transparent.

5.3 The Core Second-Best Result

AUTHOR'S RESULT

Inspection of the implementation regions shows that no governance structure dominates every other for all joint distributions f(x,y). Conversely, each of the forms examined can be the surplus-maximizing feasible structure for some environment. Therefore the observed plethora of alliance forms is not inherently anomalous.

Different rights
Different private objective
Different threshold
Different errors vs first best
Distribution f(x,y) weights errors
Different second-best winner
DO NOT OVERSTATE

The paper does not derive a universal ranking such as “licensing beats acquisition when spillovers are high.” Without richer assumptions on spillover distributions and institutional details, the model intentionally stops short of such a prediction.

Formal-status note: the paper presents this conclusion through graphical implementation-region and expected-surplus comparisons; it does not state a separately numbered Proposition, Lemma, or Theorem.

Source: §4 · PDF pp. 24–25

5.4 What Is Needed for Sharp Predictions

To choose between concrete forms in a real setting, the researcher needs institutional knowledge about which decisions are noncontractible, how each decision affects each party, how state realizations change those consequences, and how likely different states are.

Biotech control illustration

If development is good for the biotech in virtually every state, giving the biotech control tends toward implementation in all states. That can be inefficient when negative spillovers on the pharmaceutical firm are large and likely.

Opposite control

Giving the pharmaceutical firm control can instead create under-implementation because it ignores the biotech's positive spillover. The right allocation depends on the distribution of spillovers.

MERGER RETURNS

Because none of the alliance forms is generally first-best, a full merger can sometimes dominate when a and b are sufficiently important relative to A and B that alliance inefficiency exceeds the integration cost of combining the parents.

Source: §4 · PDF pp. 25–26

6. Beyond Unique Control: Joint Ventures and Relationships

6.1 Joint Control and the Adaptation Problem

Sections 3–4 deliberately impose unique control: every asset or decision right belongs to exactly one party. Actual alliances often use shared control, especially joint ventures, so §5a asks what changes once several parties participate in the decision process.

49–49–2 voting example

Suppose A owns 49% of a joint venture, B owns 49%, and C owns 2%. C receives some project payoff and, because direct project payoffs are positive, always votes to proceed. Under majority voting, the project is stopped only when both A and B vote no. This produces an implementation pattern unlike the unique-control structures analyzed earlier.

SUBTLE CONTRACTIBILITY ISSUE

A binding vote over whether to implement requires that the outcome of the voting procedure itself be enforceable on the person charged with implementation. The paper therefore does not present the voting example as a complete theory of joint ventures.

State realized
Manager adapts
Board observes ex post
Board evaluates action
Rewards / sanctions
Better adaptation?

The authors' broader interpretation is that elaborate joint-venture governance may be designed to make ex post adaptation more feasible. A manager can react to the realized state, while a board evaluates the action after the fact. This requires knowledgeable board members, measurement and control systems, and incentive arrangements, so the mechanism is costly and predicted to be worthwhile only when the value of adaptation is high.

Source: §5a · PDF pp. 27–28

6.2 Relationships and Self-Enforcing Relational Contracts

A one-shot alliance cannot use future consequences to discipline noncontractible actions. Repetition changes that. If parties observe each other's behavior, a relational contract can make cooperation self-enforcing: reneging today risks destroying future surplus. This is more powerful when the “shadow of the future” is large.

1 · Long-lived alliance

Continuing interaction inside the same alliance makes current opportunism costly. The paper cites the decades-long Fuji–Xerox relationship in Asia-Pacific, including major restructurings.

2 · Repeated alliance partners

Firms can sign multiple alliances with the same counterparty, so behavior today influences future bilateral opportunities.

3 · Indirect network ties

Even firms that never contract directly may exchange information through common partners. Reputation can therefore travel through the alliance network.

MECHANISM

Observable-but-nonverifiable actions cannot be enforced by courts, but they can enter a self-enforcing informal agreement when the future value of the relationship exceeds the short-run gain from deviation.

Source: §5b · PDF pp. 28–30

8.2 What Actually Drives the Result?

Spillovers vary by state
They cannot be fully contracted
Control is fixed before state
Controller ignores some surplus
Each governance makes different errors
No universal second-best form
ANALYTICAL SYNTHESIS

The multiplicity result is driven less by investment incentives than by state-contingent adaptation under incomplete internalization. Governance is valuable because it chooses which externalities will be internalized in the states where fast decisions are required.

8.3 Authors' Limitations and Scope Conditions

Broad framework, weak generic predictions

The authors explicitly concede that the general model cannot say which form will be chosen in a particular industry without more institutional structure and a specified distribution of spillovers.

Joint control is only sketched

The one-shot model assumes unique control. Joint ventures involve procedures, managers, boards, measurement, and relational elements that require a richer theory.

Relationships are an extension

Relational contracting is discussed conceptually and linked to descriptive evidence, not fully integrated into the main governance optimization.

Biotech evidence is motivating

The database establishes prevalence and network density but does not causally test the comparative efficiency of governance structures.

8.4 Analytical Limitations

ANALYTICAL SYNTHESIS

Selection into governance: the descriptive evidence cannot distinguish whether a form causes better outcomes or is chosen because parties anticipate particular spillover states. A serious empirical test needs variation in governance or contractibility plus observed outcomes.

ANALYTICAL SYNTHESIS

Endogenous formation and investment are absent: the model starts with a potential project and focuses on implementation. It does not jointly model search for partners, alliance formation, relationship-specific investment, renegotiation, termination, or learning.

ANALYTICAL SYNTHESIS

Contractibility is coarse: real alliances contain many decision rights, milestones, vetoes, staged payments, field-of-use restrictions, option clauses, and dispute mechanisms. A vector of partially contractible decisions could generate sharper predictions.

8.5 External Validity and Competing Mechanisms

ANALYTICAL SYNTHESIS

The framework is intended to travel across industries, but what travels is the logic—not the biotech parameters. In industries where the key friction is relationship-specific investment, asymmetric information, financing, market power, or regulatory constraints, the same governance form may be chosen for reasons outside this model.

9. Research Implications / Extensions

RESEARCH EXTENSION

The items below are learning-oriented extensions derived from the framework. They are not claims the paper itself proves.

Dynamic governance transitions

Let partnerships move among procurement, collaboration, licensing, shared control, acquisition, and termination as beliefs about spillovers and adaptation needs evolve.

Endogenous partner search and matching

Allow firms to choose partners based on expected complementarity, spillover covariance, and the set of feasible governance contracts.

Bargaining over governance

Separate total-surplus-maximizing governance from bargaining over transfers and control. Outside options may affect which efficient—or inefficient—allocation is implementable.

Empirical contract design

Code real contracts into asset ownership, veto/decision rights, milestone rights, royalties, options, and termination clauses; then relate those dimensions to ex ante proxies for adaptation risk.

Network reputation

Model indirect ties explicitly so a deviation against one partner affects continuation values with others. The dense biotech network makes this mechanism empirically plausible.

Joint-venture adaptation technology

Endogenize the cost of boards, measurement systems, delegation, and expert control. Predict when parties pay for richer governance rather than assign unique control.

Q1. Why are alliances “bridges” rather than simply smaller firms?

CORE · LITERATURE
Hint: Focus on what can cross a fixed firm boundary without transferring the whole firm.
Suggested Answer: The paper treats alliances as contractual structures that can reallocate project assets, decision rights, and payoffs while the parent firms remain distinct. A bridge can therefore change control and incentives across a boundary without requiring full integration.

Common Mistake: Saying that every hybrid structure simply blurs ownership. Licensing may leave ownership fixed while moving decision rights.

Why This Matters: This is the paper's conceptual contribution to the market-versus-firm literature. Review §1.1.

Q2. What friction replaces the standard hold-up story at the center of this paper?

MECHANISM
Hint: Think about the moment after the state is known but before implementation.
Suggested Answer: State-dependent spillovers and ex post noncontractibility of decisions are central. The relevant state is observable but not verifiable, decisions cannot be contracted or renegotiated in time, so the holder of the decision right follows a private objective and ignores some external effects.

Common Mistake: Describing the paper as a model of underinvestment in relationship-specific assets. The practitioners mentioned that mechanism much less than spillovers and ex post adaptation.

Why This Matters: It identifies what is genuinely different from the canonical property-rights hold-up formulation. Review §1.2 and §3.2.

Q3. Reconstruct the implementation rule under Unstructured Collaboration.

FORMAL MODEL
Hint: Both A and B must agree. Ask what each privately receives if the project proceeds.
Suggested Answer: With A owning a and B owning b, A agrees iff πA(s)+πa>0 and B agrees iff πB(s)+πb>0. Because coordinated use needs both, implementation requires both inequalities simultaneously.

Common Mistake: Using the first-best sum πA+πB+πa+πb>0. The whole point is that no single decision maker internalizes that sum.

Why This Matters: Once you can derive this threshold, the implementation-region method becomes transparent. Review §4.1 and §5.1.

Q4. Why can Acquisition and Total Divestiture make opposite kinds of mistakes?

MECHANISM · FORMAL MODEL
Hint: List what A internalizes after acquisition and what C internalizes after total divestiture.
Suggested Answer: Acquisition by A internalizes x plus both direct payoffs but ignores y, so it may either over- or under-implement depending on B's spillover. Total divestiture gives C only positive direct project payoffs, so C always implements and therefore over-implements whenever sufficiently negative parent spillovers make total surplus negative.

Common Mistake: Assuming “more integration” monotonically means more efficiency. Each form internalizes a different subset of surplus.

Why This Matters: It explains why governance is not a one-dimensional integration scale. Review §5.2.

Q5. What does licensing add that asset ownership alone cannot do?

GOVERNANCE DESIGN
Hint: Separate the right to choose from the right to receive the asset's payoff.
Suggested Answer: Licensing can transfer a decision right without transferring ownership or the associated direct payoff. It therefore creates incentive configurations unavailable when di and πi must move together as an asset bundle.

Common Mistake: Saying the action itself becomes contractible. Only the allocation of authority is contracted; the realized decision remains noncontractible.

Why This Matters: This is the formal meaning of a contractual bridge across a fixed boundary. Review §4.2.

Q6. Why can every examined governance form be second-best while none is generally first-best?

CORE RESULT
Hint: Compare each form's implementation region with the diagonal first-best region, then think about where probability mass lies.
Suggested Answer: Each form creates a different implementation frontier because its controllers internalize different payoff components. No frontier matches the first-best total-surplus frontier in general. But the loss from each mismatch depends on f(x,y); one can place sufficient probability weight on states where a particular governance rule makes fewer costly errors than all alternatives.

Common Mistake: Treating second-best as “second most efficient” in a fixed ranking. Here it means the best feasible governance under a particular environment.

Why This Matters: This is the central theoretical conclusion. Review §5.3.

Q7. What do the biotech data establish, and what do they not establish?

EVIDENCE · IDENTIFICATION
Hint: Separate descriptive prevalence/network evidence from causal governance effects.
Suggested Answer: The data document a dense alliance network, repeated ties, and a wide range of governance forms and objectives. They motivate the feasible set and relational extension. They do not identify whether one governance form causes higher surplus or why a particular alliance selected its contract.

Common Mistake: Reading Table 2 as evidence that licensing is more efficient because it is more common.

Why This Matters: It prevents conflating the paper's empirical motivation with causal identification. Review §2.

Q8. How do Joint Control and Relational Contracts attack the same adaptation problem differently?

EXTENSION · CRITIQUE
Hint: One creates an internal decision process; the other uses future continuation value.
Suggested Answer: Joint control can build a costly institutional mechanism—manager, board, voting, measurement, evaluation—to adapt decisions to the realized state. Relational contracting instead makes observable nonverifiable behavior self-enforcing because deviation sacrifices future bilateral or network surplus. Both try to improve ex post adaptation beyond fixed one-shot control rights.

Common Mistake: Treating a joint venture simply as 50/50 asset ownership. The paper stresses the decision procedure and adaptive governance around it.

Why This Matters: These extensions point toward a richer theory of alliance governance. Review §6.

12. What Should I Remember?

8 Things to Remember

  1. Strategic alliances are modeled as allocations of assets, decision rights, and payoffs across distinct firms.
  2. The central frictions are state-dependent spillovers and noncontractible ex post decisions, not primarily ex ante hold-up.
  3. Governance is chosen before s; s is observed; controllers act; payoffs follow. There is no time for ex post recontracting.
  4. First best implements iff πA+πB+πa+πb>0.
  5. Ownership, licensing, and royalties change which payoff components enter the controller's private implementation criterion.
  6. Every form studied can be second-best for some environment; none is generally first-best.
  7. The biotech data show dense contractual networks, heterogeneous governance, long-lived and repeated ties—but not causal efficiency effects.
  8. Joint ventures and relational contracts are natural ways to tackle the remaining adaptation problem.
Authors' conclusion · the paper's three stated goals
  1. Document the plethora of governance structures that fall under strategic alliances.
  2. Build a simple framework, informed by practitioner observations, that defines and can potentially explain this variety through allocations of assets, decision rights, and payoffs.
  3. Look beyond the one-shot framework toward joint ventures and relational contracting, with the hope that richer institutional data will support more specific models and testable predictions.
Source: §6 Conclusion · PDF p. 31
One-Sentence Summary

When rapid, noncontractible decisions create externalities across firms, alliance governance chooses who will internalize which parts of surplus, so different contractual bridges are optimal in different environments.

Core Mechanism

Allocation of control/payoffs → private implementation threshold → state-contingent implementation errors → expected surplus ranking.

Most Important Result

No universal governance winner: each feasible form can be second-best for a suitable f(x,y).

Biggest Contribution

A unified language for ownership changes, licensing, royalties, collaborations, divestitures, and the path toward joint/relational governance.

Biggest Limitation

The framework is deliberately broad and therefore does not itself deliver sharp setting-free empirical predictions.

Biggest Open Question

How should rich real-world adaptation institutions and relationships be modeled and measured so governance predictions become testable?
