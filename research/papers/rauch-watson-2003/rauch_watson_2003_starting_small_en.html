Starting Small in an Unfamiliar Environment

A loss-minimized interactive reconstruction of James E. Rauch & Joel Watson (2003): why buyers use trial orders, how search frictions and uncertainty shape supplier choice, and why relationship persistence is tied to the way a match begins.
International Journal of Industrial Organization 21 (2003), 1021–1042JEL L24 · D83Paper type: Theory + empirical motivation

Paper Dashboard

Research Question
When should a developed-country buyer begin a new LDC supplier relationship with a small trial order rather than a large order or renewed search?
Core Friction
Uncertain supplier capability + irreversible buyer-funded training + costly search for alternatives.
Key Choice

1 · Research Puzzle & Literature Position

Partnerships often begin under uncertainty. The paper asks why DC buyers dealing with LDC suppliers so often start with a small order even when the formal payment environment can already protect the buyer against paying for defective delivery.

Observed practice
New LDC supplier relationships often begin with small orders.
→
Contracting puzzle
Letters of credit can let buyers refuse payment when terms are not met.
→
Missing risk
The buyer still risks an irreversible supplier-training investment.
→
Model question
When is information from a small order worth the delay?

§1, pp. 1021–1024; §2, pp. 1024–1027.

1.1 What the Paper Combines

The authors emphasize three ingredients that prior work had largely studied separately.

(a) Costly search in a heterogeneous pool

Related to frictional matching and assortative matching. Smith (1997) and Smith & Shimer (2000) study heterogeneous matching under search frictions.

(b) Different ways to build a relationship

Optimal experimentation and irreversible investment under uncertainty explain why an agent may first use a smaller experiment or contractual form before making a larger commitment.

(c) Stay or switch after success

Jovanovic (1979) has learning and turnover, but not the option to skip the learning phase and immediately make the large relationship-specific investment. Rauch & Watson add endogenous supplier switching.

Substantively discussed precursor literatures
  • Optimal experimentation: Moscarini & Smith (1998) show experimentation size can increase with optimism; in this sense a pessimistic decision maker starts small. Aghion et al. (1991), Keller & Rady (1999), Bergemann & Välimäki (2000), and Bolton & Harris (1999) are cited as related experimentation work.
  • Irreversible investment: Dixit & Pindyck (1994). Horstmann & Markusen (1996) study local sales agency versus direct investment in entering a foreign market; the agency contract generates information useful for a later direct-investment decision.
  • Incomplete information and cooperation: Ghosh & Ray (1996) and Watson (1996, 1999, 2002) obtain starting small because agents are uncertain about partners' incentives to cooperate. Watson studies gradual escalation of stakes; Ghosh & Ray include matching and myopic types. Datta (1996), Kranton (1996), and Carmichael & MacLeod (1997) are also cited for trust/discipline/cooperation roles.
  • Matching and turnover: Smith (1997), Smith & Shimer (2000), and Jovanovic (1979).

§1, pp. 1022–1024, including footnotes 1–2.

Author's argument
The novelty is the interaction of search + starting-scale choice + supplier switching, which generates predictions linking the beginning and duration of buyer–supplier relationships.

2 · Empirical Motivation

2.1 Evidence That Buyers Actually “Start Small”

Egan & Mody (1992)

Surveyed U.S. buyers operating in LDCs. Buyers often begin with small orders and let the relationship build gradually. The trial reveals whether managers and workers can meet price, quality, and delivery requirements. The survey stresses managerial capability and capacity to absorb training, not just installed technology.

§2, p. 1024.

Tewari (1999)

European buyers and Indian woolen-knitwear suppliers show a sequence of focused small-scale learning followed by volume expansion. Repeated mistakes can cause a bulk buyer to leave for another supplier.

§2, p. 1024.

The paper treats these cases as evidence that the small order is an information-producing stage used before committing to large-scale production.

2.2 Why LDC Relationships Differ from DC Relationships

A letter of credit protects the buyer against paying when quality, inspection, or delivery conditions are not satisfied. Taylor & Wiggins (1997) show that a similar domestic U.S. contracting environment can support large initial orders. Rauch & Watson therefore ask what is different in LDC sourcing.

Difference 1 — Large orders often require buyer-funded training

New LDC suppliers frequently need training in production organization, quality control, and use of new equipment. Egan & Mody report buyer-supplied experts and training in developed-country plants; Rhee, Ross-Larson & Pursell (1984) find that nearly half of surveyed Korean exporters directly benefited from technical information supplied by foreign buyers. Schmitz & Knorringa (2000) similarly document training in production and quality control.

Economic point: under the letter-of-credit payment method, the crucial exposure is not necessarily the purchase price. It is the sunk training investment the buyer cannot recover if training fails.

Pack & Page (1994) connect such training to technology transfer: purchasers may transmit tacit and sometimes proprietary knowledge from other OECD suppliers, especially in older technologies such as clothing and footwear.

Payment-method detail: why a letter of credit does not eliminate the model's risk

A letter of credit also protects the seller relative to open account because the issuing bank must pay if conditions are fulfilled; this can help the supplier obtain working capital from subcontractors. Yet none of this reimburses the buyer for supplier-specific training if the supplier proves unable to perform.

§2, pp. 1024–1026, footnote 3.

Difference 2 — Non-performance risk is higher

The supplier may lack the managerial capability to learn international quality and delivery standards. A small order lets the buyer learn about capability and willingness to learn before paying for large-order training.

The more closely the initial order resembles the eventual high-volume product, the more informative it should be, but such a trial may be more costly because it can require teaching design specifications. Rauch & Watson (1999) let the buyer choose the intensity of starting small; the published model fixes this dimension because the complication does not materially change the results.

How the paper constructs the ISO and telephone comparisons

ISO 9000: the 1998 certificate counts come from the International Organization for Standardization. Industrial-worker denominators combine 1990 industrial employment shares with 1998 economically active populations. The Europe comparison includes Eastern Europe but excludes former Soviet Union countries; Mexico is added to Central and South America for the Latin America definition. Several countries are omitted because labor-force-share data are missing.

Telephones: the paper uses 1990 because later observations are missing for many countries. Telephone counts come from Canning's infrastructure database; industrial-worker denominators again use 1990 industrial employment shares. Thirteen European countries and two Latin American countries have missing telephone observations, and the same Europe/Latin America definitions are retained.

§2, footnotes 6–7, pp. 1026–1027.

Difference 3 — Search for alternatives is more costly

Starting small delays large-order profits. Why not reject a mediocre supplier and keep searching? The answer is weaker communication and transport infrastructure, which raises alternative-supplier search costs.

2.3 Networks and Relationship Persistence

Once a buyer is in a successful LDC relationship, it gains access to a product-specific information network. New potential suppliers can then arrive without the same active search cost. The incumbent still has two advantages: training is sunk and capability uncertainty has been favorably resolved.

Nadvi (1999): Sialkot surgical instruments

In May 1994, the U.S. FDA restricted imports of Pakistani surgical instruments that failed Good Manufacturing Practices. By late 1997, 133 of roughly 300 manufacturers in the Sialkot cluster were FDA-certified, up from zero before the action. Nadvi reports that certification let some buyers loosen longstanding ties and return to arm's-length search for cheaper suppliers.

What it shows: when perceived non-performance risk falls, the informational advantage of an incumbent shrinks, so relationship persistence can fall.

§2, p. 1027.

3 · Model

3.1 Economic Environment & Information

  • Time is discrete. A DC buyer searches over an infinite pool of LDC suppliers.
  • Suppliers differ only in per-period production cost c, distributed according to μ on [c̲, c̄]. The buyer observes c immediately after locating a supplier.
  • Locating a random supplier by active search costs g.
  • A supplier can be successful or unsuccessful at fulfilling large orders to the buyer's specification. Ex ante success probability is p.
  • If successful, a large order gives gross sales z and production cost c, so current profit is z−c.
  • Large-order trade requires buyer training investment I. Training reveals whether the supplier will succeed.
  • Instead of paying I immediately, the buyer can place a small order. It yields no profit in the baseline model, but after k periods reveals whether large-order training will succeed.
  • In a successful relationship, each period a network introduces a new supplier with probability q, free of active search cost. The entrant's cost is observed and the buyer can stay or switch.
  • δ is the discount factor.
Author's qualification
The zero-yield small order is a simplification. Allowing a small order to reduce later training requirements by less than one-for-one would not qualitatively change the results.

§3.1, pp. 1028–1030, footnote 8.

3.2 Timing

1
Search / draw: pay g when unmatched, draw a supplier from μ, observe c but not large-order capability.
2
Choose entry mode: Big, Learn, or Out.
3
Big: pay I now; capability is revealed. Success creates a large-order relationship; failure returns the buyer to search next period.
4
Learn: wait k periods with a small order. If the signal is favorable, pay I and move to large orders; otherwise return to search.
5
Successful match: earn z−c. With probability q, a network supplier arrives; next period choose stay versus switch.

3.3 Big / Learn / Out

Big

Invest I immediately. With probability p, the supplier succeeds and generates v(c). With probability 1−p, training fails and the buyer returns to the pool next period.

Learn

Use a small order to learn first. After k periods, invest I only if capability is adequate. The price is delay.

Out

Reject the supplier and return to the search pool next period.

3.4 Core Equations

Values of Big, Learn, and Out — Eqs. (1)–(3)
\[u^B(c)=p\,v(c)+(1-p)\delta w-I. \tag{1}\]
\[u^L(c)=\delta^k[p(v(c)-I)+(1-p)\delta w]. \tag{2}\]
\[u^O(c)=\delta w. \tag{3}\]
What it says
Big exposes I immediately; Learn delays both upside and the training decision; Out preserves the discounted search value.
Key economic wedge
Learning first avoids the chance of paying I and then failing to obtain v(c). Its cost is the k-period delay.

§3.1, Eqs. (1)–(3), p. 1029.

Optimal entry value — Eq. (4)
\[u(c)=\max\{u^B(c),u^L(c),u^O(c)\}. \tag{4}\]

This maps a supplier with cost c into the best available entry mode.

§3.1, Eq. (4), p. 1029.

Value of random search — Eq. (5)
\[w=\begin{cases}\mathbb E_{c\sim\mu}[u(c)]-g,&\mathbb E_{c\sim\mu}[u(c)]>g,\\[4pt]0,&\text{otherwise (no search).}\end{cases}\tag{5}\]

The outside option is endogenous: search is worthwhile only if the expected value of a draw exceeds its cost.

§3.1, Eq. (5), p. 1029.

3.5 Cutoffs, Figure 1, and the Switching Rule

The slope ordering is

\[\frac{\partial u^O}{\partial c}=0>\frac{\partial u^L}{\partial c}>\frac{\partial u^B}{\partial c}.\]

Low-cost suppliers generate enough upside to justify risking training immediately; intermediate-cost suppliers are worth learning about but not worth immediate exposure to I; high-cost suppliers are rejected.

\[\begin{aligned}&\text{Big: }c\in[\underline c,c^B),\\&\text{Learn: }c\in[c^B,c^L),\\&\text{Out: }c\in[c^L,\bar c].\end{aligned}\tag{7}\]
Qualification: outside the interior configuration, one or both cutoffs can lie outside the support \([\underline c,\bar c]\). In that case one or more of Big, Learn, and Out may be absent from the equilibrium choices realized in the supplier pool.

Economic intuition
With rare network arrivals, current supplier quality matters much more than future free alternatives. The support can contain low-cost Big, intermediate Learn, and high-cost Out regions.

Proposition 3 · Higher search cost expands LearnComparative statics

If q is sufficiently small, an increase in g causes \(c^B\downarrow\) and \(c^L\uparrow\).

Why cᴸ rises
Returning to the pool is less attractive, so the buyer learns about higher-cost suppliers rather than rejecting them.
Why cᴮ falls
Big also carries a failure branch that returns the buyer to costly search. Higher g therefore makes risky Big less attractive relative to delayed Learn.

§4.1, p. 1032; footnote 11.

Proposition 4 · Lower success probability expands LearnComparative statics

If q is sufficiently small and g sufficiently close to the search threshold, a decrease in p causes \(c^B\downarrow\) and \(c^L\uparrow\).

Intuition: lower p raises the chance that immediate training I is wasted—the exact loss Learn is designed to avoid.

Technical intuition from footnote 12

At q=0, v(c) is independent of p. Lower p directly harms Big more than Learn and also depresses w. Because Learn delays the random return to the pool, this indirect effect also favors Learn relative to Big. Near the no-search threshold, Learn falls more slowly than Out at the Learn/Out cutoff, so cL rises.

§4.1, pp. 1032–1033, footnote 12.

Proposition 5 · Higher training cost shrinks Big, but Learn is ambiguousComparative statics

An increase in I causes \(c^B\downarrow\).

The paper deliberately does not claim that higher I always raises the probability of Learn. If I=0, Learn is never chosen, so training cost is essential to the motive for learning. Yet higher I also makes the whole supplier pool less attractive and can make Out more attractive relative to Learn, potentially lowering cL.

Qualification: “more at stake” does not mechanically imply “more experimentation.” The endogenous outside search value can move the Learn/Out boundary the other way.

§4.1, p. 1033, footnote 13.

4.2 Persistence of Relationships — Propositions 6–9

Proposition 6 · Less caution toward entrants shortens relationshipsFormal result

If q is small, an increase in p or a decrease in I causes \(c^*(c)\) to rise weakly for all incumbent costs.

A new supplier no longer needs to be as much cheaper to offset uncertainty and retraining. Switching becomes easier, reducing expected relationship duration.

§4.2, p. 1034, footnote 14.

Proposition 7 · Big-start relationships last longerTestable prediction

Relationships initiated with a large order are more persistent than relationships that began small.

Mechanism: Big is selected for lower-cost suppliers, which are harder for entrants to beat.

§4.2, p. 1034.

Proposition 8 · Switch-created matches are more likely to start BigTestable prediction

The Big share is higher among relationships formed by switching from an existing supplier than among relationships formed by unmatched buyers' search.

Mechanism: an incumbent is abandoned only for a sufficiently low-cost entrant; switch-created matches are selected toward low c, where Big is optimal.

§4.2, pp. 1034–1035.

Proposition 9 · Longer old relationships predict Big with the next supplierTestable prediction

Relationship duration is positively correlated with the probability that, after switching, the buyer starts Big with the new supplier.

Mechanism: a long-lived incumbent is likely low-cost, so an entrant that displaces it must be even lower-cost.

§4.2, p. 1035.

Evidence status
The paper says evidence in §2 is supportive of Propositions 3–6. For Propositions 7–9, the authors report no evidence of the cited type that supports or contradicts them; they are new survey-testable predictions.

4.3 Results with More Structure on Per-Period Costs

The baseline model does not specify why supplier costs differ. Additional empirical predictions depend on the source of heterogeneity.

If cost differences mainly reflect management quality

Within a country with roughly common factor prices, better management can be treated as a Hicks-neutral productivity shifter. Since Big is associated with lower c, input demand per unit of output should be lower in relationships that began Big.

If cost differences mainly reflect factor prices

The prediction reverses: low monetary cost can reflect cheaper inputs rather than greater physical productivity, so Big-start relationships may use more inputs per unit of output.

The authors also warn against inferring that lower-factor-price countries must have more Big. Lower factor prices can shift the cost distribution down, but such countries may simultaneously have higher search costs and lower success probabilities, both of which favor Learn.

§4.3, p. 1035.

5 · Appendix Proof Architecture

The appendix does more than provide algebra: it shows which restrictions make the comparative statics work. The key recurring device is to separate the value of an incumbent successful match, \(v\), from the value of drawing or trying a new supplier, \(u\) and \(w\).

5.1 Proposition 1 — Fixed Point and Search Threshold

APPENDIX

Define \(f(y,x,c')\) as the maximum of the three continuation choices when a candidate continuation-value function is \(y\) and the pool value is \(x\). Then define an operator \(F=(F_1,F_2)\), where \(F_1\) reproduces the successful-relationship Bellman equation and \(F_2\) reproduces the search-value equation.

\[ \|F(y,x)-F(y',x')\| \le [p+(1-p)\delta]\, \|(y,x)-(y',x')\|. \]

Because \(p+(1-p)\delta<1\), \(F\) is a contraction under the sup norm. Hence the fixed point is unique. The second component falls with \(g\), while both components are increasing in \(x\), which yields a critical search cost separating \(w>0\) from \(w=0\).

Source: Appendix, Proof of Proposition 1, pp. 1037–1038.

5.2 Proposition 2 — Why an Interior Big/Learn/Out Region Exists

The proof takes the transparent limiting case \(q=0\) and sets \(g\) at the search threshold, so \(w=0\) and \(v(c)=(z-c)/(1-\delta)\). The two cutoffs become:

\[ c^{B} = z-\frac{I(1-\delta^k p)(1-\delta)} {p(1-\delta^k)}, \qquad c^{L}=z-I(1-\delta). \]

Assumption (A) puts these cutoffs strictly inside the support and gives \(c^B<c^L\). Continuity then supports the stated result for \(q\) sufficiently close to zero and \(g\) sufficiently close to the threshold.

Source: Appendix, Proof of Proposition 2, p. 1038.

5.3 Propositions 3–6 — Direct vs. Indirect Effects

Proposition 3

The appendix introduces another contraction operator \(G\) for \(v\), treating \(w\) as an argument. As \(q\to0\), changes in \(w\) induced by \(g\) have an arbitrarily small effect on \(v\). Thus a higher \(g\) mainly lowers the attractiveness of returning to the pool, which widens the Learn region.

Proposition 4

Derivative comparisons are evaluated at \(c^B\) and \(c^L\). Near the no-search boundary, a fall in \(p\) hurts Big more than Learn at the first cutoff and makes Learn decline more slowly than Out at the second cutoff.

Proposition 5

At \(c^B\), an increase in \(I\) reduces Big relative to Learn, so \(c^B\) falls. The appendix does not establish a corresponding monotone result for \(c^L\), matching the paper's caution about the overall propensity to Learn.

Proposition 6

An increase in \(p\) or decrease in \(I\) raises both \(u\) and \(v\), but when \(q\) is small the direct effect on a prospective supplier's \(u\) dominates the indirect effect on incumbent \(v\). Since \(u\) decreases in cost, equality in (8) requires a higher \(c^*(c)\).

Source: Appendix, Proofs of Propositions 3–6, pp. 1038–1040.

5.4 Propositions 7–9 — Truncated Cost Distributions

The last three proofs exploit selection. Relationships formed through unmatched search draw costs conditional on \(c<c^L\). A buyer switching from an incumbent accepts only \(c'<c^*(c)\), and \(c^*(c)<c^L\). Therefore switched-to suppliers are selected from a lower-cost truncation of \(\mu\), increasing the probability of Big. Because longevity is negatively associated with incumbent cost, a longer-lived incumbent also implies a more demanding cutoff for the replacement supplier, generating Proposition 9. Proposition 7 follows because switching probability rises with current supplier cost.

Source: Appendix, Proof of Propositions 7–9, p. 1040.

6 · Exporting, Trade Costs, and Development Interpretation

6.1 Buyer–Supplier Formation as Export Selection

A successful DC–LDC supplier relationship generally produces exports from the LDC to the DC. This lets Rauch and Watson connect their model to firm-level exporter evidence. Studies such as Aw, Chung, and Roberts (2000) and Clerides, Lach, and Tybout (1998) find that more efficient firms are more likely to become exporters. The model is consistent with that selection pattern because the buyer rejects sufficiently high-cost suppliers and accepts lower-cost suppliers through Big or Learn.

Source: Section 5, pp. 1036–1037.

6.2 Lower Search Costs as an Analogue of Trade Liberalization

The paper interprets a reduction in \(g\) as analogous to lower trade barriers. In Proposition 3, lower \(g\) narrows the Learn region: the buyer is more willing to search again rather than nurture an unattractive candidate, while low-cost candidates are more likely to receive a large order immediately. Rauch and Watson connect this sorting effect to the “rationalization” findings of Muendler (2002) and Pavcnik (2000), in which trade liberalization accelerates expansion of more efficient firms and exit of less efficient firms.

Source: Section 5, p. 1036.

6.3 A Testable Export-Dynamics Implication

AUTHOR'S SUGGESTION

The paper proposes testing whether less efficient firms that nevertheless enter export markets display more gradual export-volume expansion than more efficient entrants. That prediction is the export-market counterpart of Learn versus Big.

The closing footnote also situates these relationships in global production: Gereffi (1995) distinguishes export-processing assembly, component-supply subcontracting, original equipment manufacturing, and original brand-name manufacturing. Rauch and Watson note that only the last need not involve DC firms and report that developing-country manufactured goods rose from 4.8% of developed-market-economy imports in 1970 to 14.5% in 1991.

Source: Section 5 and footnote 15, pp. 1036–1037.

8 · Critical Reading

8.1 Key Assumptions

  • A supplier's large-order cost \(c\) is observed immediately, but its ability to succeed after training is initially unknown.
  • Success probability \(p\) is common ex ante; supplier heterogeneity enters through \(c\), not through supplier-specific \(p\).
  • The small order yields information but no direct profit, and it resolves the relevant uncertainty after exactly \(k\) periods.
  • The large-order training expenditure \(I\) is irreversible and is lost if training does not succeed.
  • Free candidate arrivals occur only while a successful relationship is active, with probability \(q\) each period.
  • Several propositions require \(q\) to be sufficiently small, and Proposition 4 also requires \(g\) near the no-search threshold.

Source: Sections 3.1–4.2 and Assumption (A), pp. 1028–1034.

8.2 What Actually Drives the Results?

Learn exists because information can be acquired before \(I\).

If the small order did not reveal whether training would work, its central insurance value would disappear.

Big is selected for low \(c\).

A very attractive supplier makes delay costly enough that the buyer accepts the risk of losing \(I\).

Out is governed by the value \(w\) of returning to the pool.

Search cost therefore moves both cutoffs, not just the reject/accept margin.

Persistence results are selection results.

Low-cost suppliers both induce Big and make replacement harder, mechanically linking initiation mode and duration.

8.3 Authors' Qualifications and Limitations

  • The paper explicitly notes that the effect of \(I\) on the probability of Learn is ambiguous: Proposition 5 establishes only that \(c^B\) falls.
  • Predictions about input demand depend on the source of cost heterogeneity; management productivity and factor-price heterogeneity imply opposite signs.
  • A low-factor-price country need not generate more Big because such countries may simultaneously have higher search costs and lower success probabilities.
  • The paper reports supportive evidence for Propositions 3–6 but says it knows of no evidence, of the type discussed in Section 2, that supports or contradicts Propositions 7–9.

Source: Sections 4.1–4.3, pp. 1033–1035.

8.4 Analytical Limitations ANALYTICAL SYNTHESIS

These are limitations visible from the model structure rather than additional claims by the authors.

  • One-dimensional observed heterogeneity: all supplier heterogeneity relevant to immediate sorting is compressed into \(c\). Capability uncertainty is summarized by a common \(p\).
  • Exogenous learning technology: the buyer chooses Big versus a fixed small experiment, but not the scale, content, or intensity of learning in the published model. Footnote 4 points to Rauch and Watson (1999), where intensity is allowed.
  • No strategic supplier investment or bargaining: the supplier does not choose effort, co-investment, prices, or relationship-specific adaptation in response to governance.
  • Network arrival is reduced form: \(q\) does not arise from endogenous network formation or a two-sided matching market.
  • Duration is endogenous only through replacement opportunities: there is no exogenous match destruction or changing supplier productivity.

8.5 External Validity

The institutional evidence is concentrated in developed-country buyers sourcing from developing-country suppliers, with examples from apparel/knitwear, manufacturing, Korean exporters, Pakistani surgical instruments, and related buyer-driven production settings. The mechanism travels most naturally to relationships where a buyer must make a substantial sunk training/adaptation investment and can run an informative low-stakes trial first. It is less directly applicable where capability is certified ex ante, production is fully standardized, or the small project is uninformative about large-scale performance.

Source basis: Section 2, pp. 1024–1028; applicability statement is analytical synthesis.

8.6 Alternative Mechanisms

Rauch and Watson distinguish their mechanism from prior “starting small” theories. In Ghosh–Ray and Watson's incomplete-information work, gradual escalation can arise because partners are uncertain about cooperative incentives or types. Here the focal uncertainty is whether training will enable successful large-order fulfillment. The same observed pattern—small initial transactions followed by larger ones—could therefore reflect productive learning, trust/discipline, financing constraints, or demand learning in other settings. Distinguishing them empirically requires observing what is learned, what investment is postponed, and how relationship transitions respond to search conditions and capability certification.

Source basis: Section 1, pp. 1022–1023; final distinguishing-tests sentence is analytical synthesis.

8.7 Open Questions

  • Can micro data observe both initial order size and buyer-provided training well enough to directly identify the Learn mechanism?
  • Do capability certifications or standardized production technologies raise \(c^*(c)\) and shorten relationships as Proposition 6 predicts?
  • Are relationships that start Big empirically more persistent after conditioning for observables that proxy supplier cost?
  • How would endogenous supplier investment, bargaining, or relationship-specific technology accumulation alter the cutoff logic?
  • Can buyer-network data make \(q\) measurable rather than reduced-form?

The first three are close empirical implementations of the paper's predictions; the last two are research-oriented analytical extensions.

9 · Research Implications / Extensions

RESEARCH EXTENSION

9.1 Endogenize Relationship Depth

Replace the binary Big/Learn choice with a continuous relationship-depth or order-size decision. Training intensity, customization, co-development, or control rights could evolve after learning. This would turn the paper's threshold logic into a dynamic governance path rather than a one-time experimentation choice.

RESEARCH EXTENSION

9.2 Two-Sided Search, Bargaining, and Switching

Make suppliers strategic: they can search for buyers, make relationship-specific investments, and bargain over surplus. Then the incumbent advantage generated by sunk training can interact with outside options on both sides.

RESEARCH EXTENSION

9.3 Empirical Transition Matrix

With buyer–supplier transaction data, classify relationships by initial order scale and subsequent expansion, persistence, and replacement. The model gives a set of linked restrictions: initial Big should select lower-cost suppliers, Big relationships should last longer, and replacements after long relationships should be especially likely to start Big.

RESEARCH EXTENSION

9.4 Certification as a Shock to Uncertainty

The Nadvi GMP episode suggests a research design: a credible capability certification can be interpreted as reducing uncertainty about success. The model predicts less need to protect sunk training through incumbent relationships and thus easier switching. A modern setting with staggered certification or regulation could test this comparative static.

CORE

Q1. Why does a buyer ever start small instead of either ordering big immediately or rejecting the supplier?

Hint: Compare what is learned before the irreversible training expenditure \(I\).
Suggested Answer: A small order postpones \(I\) while revealing whether training will lead to successful large-order fulfillment. Common Mistake: Treating the small order mainly as a cheap production order. In the baseline model it yields zero profit; its payoff is information.

FORMAL MODEL

Q2. Reconstruct the cutoff ordering \(c^B<c^L\). What does each region mean?

Hint: Use the slope ordering of \(u^B,u^L,u^O\) in \(c\).
Suggested Answer: Big's value falls fastest with supplier cost, Learn falls more slowly, and Out is flat. Thus sufficiently low-cost suppliers justify Big; intermediate-cost suppliers justify Learn; high-cost suppliers are rejected: Big for \(c<c^B\), Learn for \(c^B\le c<c^L\), Out for \(c\ge c^L\).

Common Mistake: Reading \(c\) as capability. It is per-period production cost, observed before the relationship starts.

Why This Matters: Almost every comparative static operates by moving these cutoffs.

Review: 3.5 Cutoff Structure · Equation (7)

MECHANISM

Q3. Why does a higher search cost \(g\) expand the Learn region at both margins?

Hint: A failed Big order also sends the buyer back to the pool.
Suggested Answer: Higher \(g\) lowers the value \(w\) of returning to search. That makes Out less attractive than Learn, raising \(c^L\). It also makes Big less attractive relative to Learn because Big exposes the buyer to immediate failure and a return to the now less valuable pool; Learn delays that return and avoids risking \(I\), so \(c^B\) falls.

Common Mistake: Saying only that “search is expensive, so buyers keep suppliers.” That explains the upper margin but not why Big also shrinks.

Why This Matters: Proposition 3 is a distinctive two-margin comparative static.

Review: 4.1 Propositions 2–5

MECHANISM

Q4. Why is the effect of a larger training investment \(I\) on the overall probability of Learn ambiguous?

Hint: Proposition 5 gives a result for \(c^B\), not for both cutoffs.
Suggested Answer: Higher \(I\) clearly makes immediate Big more risky, so \(c^B\) falls. But \(I\) also lowers the value of prospective suppliers in the pool and can make Out relatively more attractive than Learn. The paper therefore cannot sign the movement of \(c^L\) in general; the total mass of Learn depends on the supplier-cost distribution.

Common Mistake: Inferring “more sunk cost always means more experimentation.” The lower cutoff moves as expected, but the upper cutoff need not.

Why This Matters: It is an important qualification to the intuitive story.

Review: Proposition 5

FORMAL MODEL

Q5. What does the switching cutoff \(c^*(c)\) mean, and why must \(c^*(c)<c\)?

Hint: Compare an incumbent whose training has succeeded with a new supplier of the same cost.
Suggested Answer: \(c^*(c)\) is the highest new-supplier cost at which the buyer is indifferent between the incumbent and starting with the new candidate: \(v(c)=u(c^*(c))\). A new supplier with the same cost is worse because its capability is unresolved and the buyer must again face training cost \(I\); hence a replacement must be strictly cheaper, so \(c^*(c)<c\).

Common Mistake: Treating switching as cost comparison \(c'<c\) only. The incumbent has a relational information/sunk-training advantage.

Why This Matters: This cutoff connects the starting-small model to relationship persistence.

Review: Equation (8)

EVIDENCE

Q6. How does the Sialkot GMP episode map into Proposition 6?

Hint: Certification changes the buyer's uncertainty about new suppliers.
Suggested Answer: GMP certification made new Pakistani surgical-instrument suppliers more credible on quality assurance, which can be read as increasing perceived success probability \(p\). Proposition 6 says a higher \(p\) raises \(c^*(c)\), so a new supplier needs a smaller cost advantage to displace an incumbent. Nadvi reports that certification let some buyers loosen longstanding ties and return to arm's-length search.

Common Mistake: Claiming the episode is a formal causal test. The paper presents it as supportive empirical motivation, not as an identified estimate.

Why This Matters: It shows how an institutional change maps onto a model parameter and a persistence prediction.

Review: 2.3 Networks and Persistence · Proposition 6

MECHANISM

Q7. Why should relationships that start Big last longer than those that start small?

Hint: Big is not randomly assigned across suppliers.
Suggested Answer: Big is chosen for lower-cost suppliers. A lower incumbent cost makes it harder for a newly introduced supplier to clear the switching threshold, so replacement is less likely. The duration result is therefore generated by selection on \(c\), not by a direct causal effect of making a large first order.

Common Mistake: Saying that Big itself creates durability. Proposition 7 follows because both Big and persistence are associated with low \(c\).

Why This Matters: It prevents a causal misreading of one of the paper's most testable predictions.

Review: Proposition 7 · Appendix proof

CRITIQUE

Q8. What observed “starting small” pattern would not, by itself, identify Rauch–Watson's mechanism?

Hint: Section 1 discusses several other reasons relationships may escalate gradually.
Suggested Answer: Simply observing small initial transactions followed by larger ones is not sufficient. Similar dynamics can arise from uncertainty about cooperative behavior, trust building, demand experimentation, or other irreversible-investment problems. Evidence for this paper's mechanism should connect initial scale to buyer-provided training, learning about productive capability, search costs, and switching responses to changes in capability uncertainty.

Common Mistake: Treating the stylized fact as mechanism-specific.

Why This Matters: It is the key empirical identification challenge if the theory is taken to data.

12 · What Should I Remember?

Eight Things to Remember

  1. Puzzle: buyers may start with small orders even when contracts protect them from paying for defective deliveries because buyer-funded training is still at risk.
  2. Core friction: the buyer observes cost \(c\) but is uncertain whether training will make the supplier capable of fulfilling large orders.
  3. Three choices: Big risks \(I\) now, Learn buys information before \(I\), Out returns to search.
  4. Sorting: low \(c\) → Big; intermediate \(c\) → Learn; high \(c\) → Out.
  5. Search cost: higher \(g\) widens Learn from both sides (\(c^B\downarrow,c^L\uparrow\)).
  6. Uncertainty: lower \(p\) also favors Learn under the paper's stated conditions; higher \(I\) definitely reduces Big but does not monotonically increase Learn.
  7. Persistence: incumbent advantage comes from resolved uncertainty and sunk training; higher \(p\) or lower \(I\) makes switching easier.
  8. Selection: Big relationships last longer and switched-to suppliers more often start Big because Big selects lower-cost suppliers.

One-Sentence Summary

A buyer facing costly supplier search and uncertainty about whether relationship-specific training will work may use a small trial order to acquire information before committing, and this same selection-and-learning structure determines which relationships persist or are replaced.

Core Mechanism

Uncertain capability + irreversible training + costly rematching → value of experimentation → Big/Learn/Out cost cutoffs → persistence and switching selection.

Most Important Formal Object

The three value functions (1)–(3) and their cutoff crossings in (7); equation (8) then carries the same logic into supplier replacement.

Biggest Contribution

Combining search, endogenous relationship-building mode, and supplier switching in one tractable model that generates linked predictions about initial order size and relationship duration.

Biggest Limitation

The learning technology and supplier side are highly reduced-form; several comparative statics rely on small \(q\) and, for Proposition 4, \(g\) near the search threshold.

Biggest Open Question

Can transaction-level data separately identify learning about productive capability from other reasons for gradual relationship escalation?
