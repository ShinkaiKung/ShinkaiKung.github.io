Vertical Integration and Firm Boundaries: The Evidence

Francine Lafontaine & Margaret Slade build an empirical map of two questions: what determines whether transactions occur inside firms, and what vertical integration does to prices, quantities, costs, investment, profits, rivals, and consumers.

FULL-COVERAGE reconstruction · forward integration · make-or-buy · Moral Hazard · Transaction Cost Economics · Property Rights · market power · econometrics · policy

Paper at a Glance

Boundary choice: incentives dominate the forward-retailing evidence; transaction costs dominate backward make-or-buy. Consequences: integration can generate market-power concerns, but the empirical net effects are more often efficiency-enhancing than harmful. Methodological hinge: governance is endogenous, so causal interpretation depends heavily on research design.

AUTHOR'S ARGUMENTThe paper is not a single-theory survey. Its distinctive goal is cross-fertilization: evidence originally designed to test one theory can sometimes discriminate among other theories of firm boundaries.
Source: Abstract; §1 · pp. 629–631

I · The Vertical-Integration Decision

The authors define the empirical contrast pragmatically: under vertical integration, ownership is joint and control rights are integrated; under market organization, ownership and control are separate. They acknowledge long-term contracts and “almost integration,” but generally follow the institutional definitions used by the empirical studies.

IMPORTANT DISTINCTIONAgency theory identifies integration through residual claims; Property Rights Theory through asset ownership and residual control rights. The empirical category “integrated” is therefore conceptually theory-dependent even when the data use one institutional indicator.
Source: §2 · p. 631 and note 4

Forward Integration into Retailing

The typical choice is whether a manufacturer / franchisor sells through company-owned outlets or independent retailers / franchisees. Most franchisors mix both forms. The recurring stylized facts are: more important retailer effort and greater geographic dispersion are associated with more franchising; larger outlets and more important franchisor / brand inputs are associated with more company ownership; risk is associated with more franchising, contrary to the baseline insurance prediction.

Source: §2.1 · pp. 632–633

The Moral-Hazard Model

A manufacturer M and retailer R both exert unobservable effort. A linear compensation share α determines the retailer's residual claim: α≈0 resembles a salaried employee inside the firm; α≈1 resembles an independent residual claimant. The model trades off effort incentives against insurance.

Baseline assumptions & incentive constraintsMODEL
  • The manufacturer is risk neutral; the retailer is risk averse with CARA utility. Both effort choices are unobservable.
  • The linear compensation rule is s(q)=αq+W. At α=0 the retailer is a salaried employee; at α=1 the retailer is the residual claimant.
  • Effort costs are quadratic, c(aᵢ)=aᵢ²/2. The first-best effort levels are aᵢ**=βᵢ, while incentive compatibility gives aM*=βM(1−α) and aR*=βRα.
  • A fixed contracting / administration cost T is what makes the discrete integration choice nontrivial: without it, integration is only a limiting case in this skeleton.

Moral-Hazard Evidence

Downstream effort is the cleanest success: every statistically significant estimate has the predicted negative sign. Upstream effort also performs well: stronger brand / franchisor inputs predict more integration. Monitoring initially looks contradictory, but becomes coherent once the authors distinguish outcome monitoring from direct behavior monitoring. The major failure is risk.

THE RISK PUZZLEThe baseline agency model predicts more integration as risk rises because insurance becomes more valuable. Table 1 instead shows that increased risk is generally associated with less integration. Proposed explanations include endogenous sales variability, sorting by risk preferences, and a Property Rights interpretation in which uncertainty raises the value of local discretion / investment.

Why the two monitoring results are not contradictory MECHANISM

Outcome monitoring: when sales are a poor signal of individual effort—because results are difficult to attribute, selling cycles are long, or nonselling activities matter—the firm relies less on sales-based incentives. This pushes governance toward more integration.

Behavior monitoring: direct observation of cleanliness, quality, hours, or other actions is a substitute for high-powered sales incentives. When direct behavior monitoring is easier (for example, outlets are dense or on-board computers improve observability), integration becomes more attractive; equivalently, a higher cost of behavior monitoring predicts less integration.

Why the authors use this distinction: empirical papers had appeared to make opposite claims about “monitoring costs.” Once the information generated by monitoring is separated into outcome versus behavior signals, the results line up with the agency logic.

Spillovers within a chain: what the cases are doing CASES

Brand-demand spillover: a price or quality choice at one outlet can shift demand for other outlets carrying the same brand. The integrated chain internalizes effects that an individual franchisee does not.

Free riding on the trademark: a franchisee with high-powered incentives may save private costs by using lower-quality inputs or violating chain standards, while part of the damage falls on the common brand. This makes integration or other controls potentially valuable.

Non-repeat / transient customers: the free-riding problem should be especially severe where customers are unlikely to return, such as highway locations. Table 6 is mixed, and the authors stress that approved-supplier rules, advertising requirements, and large multiunit franchisees can partially internalize these externalities without full integration.

Source: §2.1.2, “Spillovers Within a Chain” · pp. 644–647 · Table 6
Multitasking: preserve the paper's separate empirical illustrations CASES
  • Slade (1996), gasoline retailing: when the second activity is auto repair—less complementary with gasoline sales than a convenience store—vertical integration of gasoline selling is less likely. The result matches the idea that incentives on one task should depend on how tasks interact.
  • Baker & Hubbard (2003), trucking: on-board computers improve coordination and reduce the cost of multitasking between shipping and cargo handling; adoption is associated with more integration, especially where multitasking matters.
  • Azoulay (2004), pharmaceuticals: firms outsource clinical trials that are mainly data-intensive but keep trials that are also knowledge-intensive in house, consistent with balancing incentives across multiple tasks.
  • Nickerson & Silverman (2003) and Lafontaine & Shaw (2005): the positive association between brand value and integration can also be read as a multitask result: local profit incentives must be balanced against protection of the common brand.
  • Yeap (2006), restaurant chains: more complex onsite food production and table service are associated with more company ownership; failures on some tasks can damage the whole chain, making high-powered local incentives less attractive.
Source: §2.1.2, “Multiple Tasks” · pp. 645–647

Multitasking and spillovers

Chain-level externalities can make integration attractive because a company-owned outlet internalizes effects on the common brand, but the non-repeat-business evidence is mixed and alternative contractual controls can mitigate free riding. Multitask models add another reason for lower-powered incentives inside firms: when tasks interact, strong incentives on one measurable activity can distort effort away from other activities. Slade (1996), Baker & Hubbard (2003), Azoulay (2004), Nickerson & Silverman (2003), Lafontaine & Shaw (2005), and Yeap (2006) provide examples consistent with this logic.

SECTION VERDICTThe authors judge the moral-hazard framework highly successful on downstream effort, upstream effort, and monitoring; more specification-sensitive on size, spillovers, and multitasking; and clearly challenged by the empirical risk sign.
Source: §2.1.2 summary · pp. 647–648

Backward Integration / Make-or-Buy

The backward-integration literature asks whether a manufacturer produces an input in house or purchases it from a supplier. Empirically, this literature is dominated by Transaction Cost Economics (TCE), with a smaller body of direct work on Property Rights Theory (PR).

Source: §2.2 · p. 648

Transaction Cost Economics

TCE starts from relationship-specific investment. An asset has greater value inside a particular relationship than in alternative uses. Once investment is sunk, the parties are locked in and quasi-rents appear. Because contracts are incomplete, each side may haggle, renegotiate, or behave opportunistically to appropriate those quasi-rents: the hold-up problem.

Specific investment
Quasi-rent / lock-in
Incomplete contract
Haggling / hold-up
Integration more attractive
TCE PREDICTIONIn-house production is more likely when transactions are specific, complex, uncertain, involve durable or hard-to-verify assets, and generate large quasi-rents.

The authors emphasize a conceptual gap: TCE commonly assumes hold-up is mitigated inside the firm, but is often less explicit about the internal mechanism. Employees can also bargain, hold up, or engage in influence activities. The claim is comparative: internal organization is presumed to mitigate these hazards better than market contracting in the relevant settings.

Source: §2.2.1 · pp. 649–650

Property Rights Theory

PR theory shifts attention from ex post transaction costs to how ownership changes ex ante noncontractible investment incentives. Ownership determines residual control rights and therefore disagreement payoffs in later bargaining. The parties anticipate those bargaining positions when they invest.

Timing and ownership logic

t = 0
Choose asset ownership: A=1 manufacturer ownership (integration), A=0 supplier ownership (separation)
t = 1 · ex ante
Manufacturer and supplier make noncontractible investments iM, iS
t = 2 · ex post
Bargain over joint surplus; ownership changes disagreement payoffs / threat points
MECHANISMEven though ex post bargaining is efficient and a bargain is reached, anticipated disagreement payoffs distort ex ante investment. The paper uses a Nash bargaining solution with side payments. Underinvestment is typical under both ownership regimes; integration is chosen only when its pattern of investment distortions yields the higher second-best surplus.

TCE ≠ PRTCE tends to treat a rise in specificity / quasi-rents as making integration more attractive. PR says the sign depends on whose marginal investment productivity or disagreement payoff changes. For example, a rise in the supplier’s contribution to joint surplus can make supplier ownership—and therefore separation—more attractive.

The paper also identifies a useful bridge between PR and Moral Hazard: both frameworks often predict that integration becomes more likely when the manufacturer’s productive input becomes more important and less likely when the other party’s input becomes more important. The objects differ—effort and residual claims in MH; asset investment and residual control rights in PR—but the comparative statics can line up.

Source: §2.2.2 · pp. 650–653

Evidence on TCE Predictions

The empirical regularity is exceptionally strong. Physical, human, dedicated, temporal, and general specificity usually predict more integration. Site specificity is less conclusive but its only significant result is positive. Complexity and uncertainty also generally predict more integration. Measurement is difficult because public data rarely observe specificity or contractual complexity; many studies rely on interviews, questionnaires, qualitative indices, or context-specific proxies.

AUTHOR'S VERDICTFor backward manufacturer–supplier relationships, “the weight of the evidence is overwhelming”: almost no statistically significant results contradict TCE predictions.

Property Rights Evidence & Cross-Fertilization

Direct PR tests are sparse, and the authors reject the common shortcut of reading every TCE test as a PR test. When predictions conflict, standard backward-integration evidence usually looks more TCE-friendly. Two important exceptions point toward PR: Acemoglu et al. (2005) find upstream technological intensity associated with less integration, while Woodruff (2002) finds a forward-integration result inconsistent with simple TCE.

The authors then reinterpret the franchising evidence. Variables used as proxies for retailer effort can also be seen as proxies for the productivity of retailer human-capital investment; their negative relation with integration is compatible with PR. The “risk puzzle” can also be read through PR: greater uncertainty may make local retailer flexibility, inventory management, and market-specific knowledge more productive, increasing the value of retailer control and separation.

CROSS-FERTILIZATIONEvidence need not stay inside the theoretical box in which it was originally estimated. The paper’s most distinctive move is to reuse MH and TCE empirical regularities to ask what they imply for PR.

A further under-tested implication shared by TCE and PR is that specificity should matter especially when contracting is incomplete. Yet interaction terms such as specificity × complexity or specificity × uncertainty are rarely tested directly. Masten (1984) and Anderson (1985) are exceptions.

Source: §2.2.4 · pp. 658–660

Econometric Issues in the Integration Decision

Many studies use outlet- or transaction-level binary integration choices. Standard Logit / Probit methods are available, but three recurring problems complicate inference.

EndogeneityFirm age, size and outlet characteristics are partly managerial choices and can move jointly with governance.
HeteroskedasticityWith Probit, heteroskedastic errors can make estimates inconsistent, not merely inefficient.
Spatial dependenceOutlets in common markets or chains can share shocks / information, generating correlated errors.

A linear probability model with 2SLS can be convenient when valid instruments exist, but instruments are notoriously difficult to find and LPMs bring their own functional-form limitations. The authors’ defense of the literature is therefore cumulative rather than study-by-study: sign consistency across many industries, measures, and methods can be persuasive even when individual designs are imperfect.

Source: §2.3 · pp. 660–662

II · Consequences of Vertical Integration

The second half asks whether integration changes prices, quantities, costs, profits, investment and consumer welfare. Efficiency theories alone would make restrictive merger policy difficult to justify, so the authors add market-power motives—especially exclusion and collusion—and then ask what the evidence actually shows.

KEY POLICY LOGICA vertical merger can be chosen for strategic reasons and still help consumers. Conversely, evidence that rivals are disadvantaged is not by itself evidence that consumers lose.
Source: §3 opening · pp. 662–663

CONTRACTUAL SUBSTITUTESTwo-part tariffs, maximum resale prices, quantity forcing and tying can reproduce some outcomes of integration. This creates both an empirical identification problem and a policy-substitution problem: banning the merger may simply redirect the firm toward a vertical restraint.

Methodological Issues: Estimating Consequences

Here integration is explicitly a treatment, but firms choose that treatment. The counterfactual outcome for an integrated firm had it remained separated is unobserved, making selection central.

Equation (19) · Ideal treatment effect
Δ* = ȳ_v,T − ȳ_vm,T
What it says
The object of interest compares observed performance of integrated firms with the unobserved performance those same firms would have had under market organization.
Role in the paper
Makes the missing-counterfactual problem explicit.

Identification details the survey emphasizes

Cross-section, time series, panel & DDIDENTIFICATION

Descriptive before/after patterns can establish an empirical regularity, but many mechanisms can generate the same pattern. Pure time-series studies confound integration with other changes over time; pure cross sections compare firms that have endogenously selected different organizational forms.

Panel data allow firm and time fixed effects. Firm fixed effects remove time-invariant firm characteristics; time fixed effects remove shocks common to all firms. Difference-in-Difference (DD) additionally compares treated and untreated changes. The paper stresses that legal restrictions can provide more exogenous organizational variation, but even law-based variation may be targeted at markets where the underlying problem is unusually severe.

Controls and IV can help, but the authors repeatedly stress the scarcity of valid instruments and the impossibility of observing every relevant control.

Source: §3.2.1 · pp. 666–669
Event-study workflow and interpretationIDENTIFICATION

Event studies require publicly traded firms and the efficient-markets assumption. The merger or policy news must be sufficiently surprising. Researchers estimate a market model in a pre-event estimation window, forecast expected returns in an event window, and compute abnormal returns as realized minus predicted returns.

The event window is a substantive choice: too narrow can miss leakage or delayed reaction; too broad can average away the effect. Acquirer, target and merged-firm returns can also move differently.

For vertical mergers, rival returns do not cleanly distinguish efficiency from foreclosure: an efficient cost reduction can hurt downstream rivals, just as raising rivals’ costs can. Looking at downstream customer firms can help, but those effects may be remote and weak.

Source: §3.2.2 · pp. 668–670
Structural / merger simulationMETHOD

Reduced-form estimates cannot by themselves recover preference and technology parameters for ex ante merger counterfactuals, which motivates structural simulation. But the survey is cautious about a generic vertical-merger simulator.

Vertical efficiencies often come from incentive alignment rather than a simple technological cost shift, and a complete model may need strategic interaction both upstream and downstream plus bargaining inside vertical structures. The specialized models the authors cite do not fit many differentiated retail settings. None of the consequence evidence summarized in Tables 15–17 is generated by a structural vertical-merger simulation.

Three empirical families

Cross section / panel / DiDDirectly compare outcomes across organizational forms or changes. Main threat: endogenous selection and weak instruments.
Event studiesUse abnormal stock returns around announcements. Main threat: events bundle information and market expectations, not only pure vertical effects.
Simulation / structural modelsCan model equilibrium counterfactuals, but vertical-merger applications were still underdeveloped in the literature reviewed.

The authors note the appeal of structural estimation in empirical IO, but emphasize that vertical applications require detailed assumptions about contracting and behavior. Their consequence evidence tables are therefore dominated by reduced-form and event-study work rather than structural merger simulations.

The paper notes that the model’s separate predictions for risk, risk aversion, and product substitutability had not been individually tested. Instead, Slade (1998b) uses retail-gasoline competitor data for a joint implication: integration should be less likely when rival price reaction functions are steep, because delegation then generates a stronger strategic response. She finds exactly that pattern. The interpretation is that separation can raise prices; firms may prefer delegation, while consumers may prefer integration.

Policy Reading

The paper traces a large swing in U.S. policy: the 1968 DOJ guidelines were suspicious of vertical mergers at relatively low market shares, the 1982/1984 guidelines treated nonhorizontal mergers as relevant mainly through horizontal effects, and the 1992 guidelines effectively focused on horizontal mergers.

What the evidence supportsDo not infer consumer harm from harm to rivals; quantify efficiencies and exclusion together; recognize that contracts can substitute for integration.
Authors’ burden-of-proof viewBecause profit-maximizing vertical integration so often appears efficient, competition authorities should provide affirmative evidence of likely harm rather than presume it.
QUALIFICATIONThis is an evidence-weight conclusion, not a theorem that vertical mergers are always benign. The authors explicitly review exclusionary mechanisms and cases in which foreclosure is observed.
Source: §3 opening; §3.3.5; §4 · pp. 662–663, 677–680

III · What the Whole Paper Says

The deepest synthesis is not “one theory wins.” It is that different institutional settings illuminate different margins. Forward retailing makes incentive allocation observable and therefore favors Moral Hazard tests. Backward input procurement exposes asset specificity and contractual incompleteness and therefore favors TCE tests. PR theory supplies sharper ownership-investment comparative statics but had far fewer direct empirical tests by 2007. Consequence studies then ask a separate welfare question where market structure and causal identification become central.

Source: §§2–4; ANALYTICAL SYNTHESIS

Critical Reading

Key assumptions and what drives results

  • Moral Hazard: output is informative about unobservable effort; residual claims affect effort; the retailer is risk averse in the baseline risk prediction.
  • TCE: incomplete contracting and specific assets create costly ex post adaptation / bargaining that internal organization mitigates relatively well.
  • PR: investment is noncontractible; ownership matters through disagreement payoffs and ex post bargaining; investment responses determine the preferred owner.
  • Consequences: organizational form is endogenous, so credible counterfactuals—not mere integrated/separated correlations—are required for welfare claims.

Authors’ limitations

  • The review largely excludes neoclassical production-function explanations based on technological economies of scale and scope.
  • It does not cover the large management literature on capabilities and resources in depth.
  • Dynamic / self-enforcing relational contracts were empirically underdeveloped relative to static boundary models.
  • The simple models abstract from internal incentives and internal hold-up, why firms rather than individuals own assets, and the ownership-control separation of the modern corporation.

Analytical limitations

ANALYTICAL SYNTHESISPublication selection is a plausible concern because the sign consistency in TCE tables is unusually strong. The authors themselves flag possible publication bias and emphasize severe measurement problems. A modern replication agenda would need preregistered designs, richer transaction-level ownership/contract data, and more explicit model comparison.

External validity and alternative mechanisms

Theories overlap: the same observed integration decision can reflect incentives, hold-up, property rights, multitasking, technology, or market power. Baker & Hubbard is used to illustrate that real institutional changes can move several mechanisms at once. This creates observational equivalence unless the researcher exploits predictions that differ across theories.

Measurement

The authors emphasize the difficulty of distinguishing vertical integration from rich contracting arrangements, measuring specific investments and complexity, and connecting average integration rates to marginal governance choices. These are not cosmetic issues: mismeasurement can turn a theoretically sharp comparative static into an ambiguous empirical proxy.

Source: §4 · pp. 678–680; measurement discussions throughout §2

Research Frontier & Extensions

AUTHOR'S FRONTIERPromising areas include dynamic relational / self-enforcing contracts, development and contracting institutions, international trade, interaction effects such as specificity × noncontractibility, and empirical work that better distinguishes overlapping theories. The conclusion also highlights complementarities among factors and among integration decisions for related components (Novak & Stern 2003): governance choices for parts of one system need not be independent.
RESEARCH EXTENSIONA natural modern extension is to model governance as a dynamic state rather than a one-shot binary choice: arm’s-length procurement → collaboration / joint investment → partial control → integration → separation. The Lafontaine–Slade evidence suggests candidate state variables—relationship-specific investment, uncertainty, monitoring technology, task interdependence, bargaining positions and market power—but the paper itself does not estimate such a dynamic transition model.
RESEARCH EXTENSIONAnother frontier is sharper empirical theory discrimination. Instead of asking only whether specificity predicts VI, seek shocks that separately move supplier versus buyer investment productivity, outside options, monitoring precision, or contractibility. These generate different signs across TCE, PR and agency models.
Source: §4 · pp. 678–680; extension boxes are explicitly marked analytical research extensions

FORMAL MODEL
Why can higher relationship specificity imply more integration under TCE but less integration under Property Rights Theory?
Show Hint: PR asks whose investment productivity changes, not only how large quasi-rents are.

Show Answer: TCE emphasizes that specificity creates quasi-rents and hold-up hazards that internal organization can mitigate, so more specificity tends to favor integration. PR instead asks how ownership changes outside options and ex ante investment incentives; if greater specificity comes from a rise in the supplier’s productive investment, supplier ownership may become more valuable and integration can fall.

Common Mistake: Treating PR as simply a formal version of TCE with identical comparative statics.

Why This Matters: This is the survey’s central theory-discrimination point.

IDENTIFICATION
Why is a cross-sectional regression of retail price on integration status not automatically causal?
Show Hint: Integration is chosen, not randomly assigned.

Show Answer: Firms select integration in response to characteristics that can also affect prices, costs, or demand. Unobserved heterogeneity and reverse adjustment of outlet characteristics can therefore contaminate the coefficient. Valid instruments or credible within-unit / policy variation are needed for a causal interpretation.

Common Mistake: Assuming controls alone eliminate endogenous organizational choice.

Why This Matters: The consequences literature faces a harder endogeneity problem than the incidence literature.

Review: consequence-methods

MECHANISM
How can vertical integration lower consumer prices under double marginalization?
Show Hint: Each separated monopoly stage adds its own markup.

Show Answer: With successive monopoly stages, each firm ignores the profit externality its markup imposes on the other stage. Integration internalizes this pricing externality and replaces multiple margins with a single joint-profit-maximizing margin, often lowering the final price.

Common Mistake: Thinking every market-power motive for integration raises prices.

Why This Matters: It explains why even an integration motivated by market power can be efficiency-enhancing.

Review: market-power

EVIDENCE
Why is evidence of foreclosure not sufficient to conclude that a vertical merger harms consumers?
Show Hint: Look at Chipty and Mullin & Mullin.

Show Answer: A merger can simultaneously foreclose or disadvantage rivals and generate efficiencies such as lower input costs or elimination of pricing distortions. Welfare depends on the net effect. Several studies find foreclosure together with efficiency gains that offset or exceed the loss.

Common Mistake: Equating harm to rivals with harm to consumers.

Why This Matters: This distinction is central to the authors’ competition-policy conclusion.

Review: consequence-evidence

CRITIQUE
Why do the authors worry about measuring average rather than marginal effects of integration?
Show Hint: A chain can contain both integrated and separated units.

Show Answer: Many data sets measure a firm’s average share integrated, while the relevant governance decision concerns the marginal transaction. Average organizational shares can hide heterogeneity across units and blur the comparative static being tested.

Common Mistake: Assuming a chain-level integration share is identical to the outlet-level make-or-buy decision.

Why This Matters: This is one reason the strong sign regularities are striking despite imperfect measurement.

What Should I Remember?

  1. The paper has two questions: what determines boundaries, and what integration does to outcomes.
  2. Forward retailing strongly supports the idea that governance allocates incentives: important retailer effort → less integration; important franchisor effort → more integration.
  3. The baseline agency prediction for risk fails: more downstream risk is usually associated with less integration.
  4. Backward make-or-buy evidence strongly supports Transaction Cost Economics: specificity, complexity and uncertainty generally predict more integration.
  5. Property Rights Theory is not just formalized TCE; ownership can move in the opposite direction depending on whose investment productivity changes.
  6. Evidence from one theory can be reinterpreted to test another—the paper calls for cross-fertilization.
  7. Vertical integration can lower prices through double marginalization even when firms have market power.
  8. Foreclosure ≠ consumer harm. Exclusion and efficiency can coexist, so welfare is a net-effect question.
  9. Estimating consequences is difficult because integration is endogenous; DiD, IV and event studies improve on naive correlations but each need assumptions.
  10. The authors’ policy reading is unusually pro-efficiency: voluntary integration often benefits consumers, whereas forced divorcement often raises prices or costs.
One-sentence summaryAcross many industries, firm boundaries systematically respond to incentive and transaction-cost frictions, while the realized effects of vertical integration are more often efficiency-enhancing than anticompetitive.
